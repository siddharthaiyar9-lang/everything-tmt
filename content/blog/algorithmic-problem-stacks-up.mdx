Contemporary discourse surrounding surveillance and digital systems
[focuses
extensively](https://eucpn.org/sites/default/files/document/files/PP%20%282%29.pdf)
on artificial intelligence. Media headlines, civil rights litigation,
and legislative debates routinely zero in on predictive policing
algorithms—software like Geolitica (formerly PredPol) or
ShotSpotter—interrogating their statistical validity and racial
biases. This focus, while well-intentioned, treats a systemic symptom as
the root cause. Essentially, these discussions tend see AI as an
autonomous entity superimposing itself onto modern law enforcement.
However, this is not the case. AI is merely the [topmost
layer](https://www.deloitte.com/global/en/industries/government-public/perspectives/urban-future-with-a-purpose/surveillance-and-predictive-policing-through-ai.html)
of a massive "stack"—which we will refer to as the "digital policing
stack"—of pre-existing digital policing infrastructure, and is
essentially the analytical component, which gives meaning to the vast
amount of data, collected through the "sensory organs," that is the
means through which such data is collected. Instead of arguing for a
traditional system of AI-regulation,

I rely on a combination of the
[techno-legal](https://psa.gov.in/CMS/web/sites/default/files/publication/AI-WP_TechnoLegal.pdf)
and
[socio-technical](https://datasociety.net/research-library/a-sociotechnical-approach-to-ai-policy/)
frameworks to argue that the challenge starts even before deployment of
AI-driven algorithmic policing (referred to interchangeably as "AI-based
policing" or "algorithmic policing") actually occurs, and that primary
focus should be on regulating the pre-existing data layer that makes
algorithmic policing possible, and not the algorithm alone. To do so, we
must first understand two things: (1) what I mean when I use the term
"digital policing," and; (2) the normative elements of it.

At its core level, digital policing refers to the integration of modern
analytical and data-driven methods into law-enforcement, characterised
by the so-called
"[dataification](https://ijlmh.com/paper/from-privacy-to-profiling-rethinking-constitutional-limits-of-state-surveillance-in-the-age-of-datafication-in-india/)"
of everyday activities such that vast amounts of information can be
easily analysed by the state. Of course, most digital infrastructures
involved in policing rely on the digitization and centralized storage of
records rather than artificial intelligence. A major example is the
[Crime and Criminal Tracking Network &
Systems](https://digitalpolice.gov.in/DigitalPolice/AboutUs) (CCTNS), a
national database that connects over 15,000 police stations to store
digitized information on complaints, active investigations, and criminal
cases.

Nonetheless, as algorithms become increasingly more powerful and
pervasive, it is set to be an inevitable inclusion in the future of
law-enforcement. As discussed, algorithms are not independent tools, but
a part of the digital policing stack, operating on top of pre-existing
digital infrastructure. For instance, since 2021, Tamil Nadu has been
using a [Facial Recognition Software
(FRS)](https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/tamil-nadu/2021/Oct/05/tamil-nadus-facial-recognition-tech-to-help-police-nab-criminals-2367709.html)
that is integrated with the CCTNS to match video feeds against digitized
criminal photographs. Similarly, the [Organised Crime Network
Database](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2200966&reg=3&lang=1)
(OCND) operates as a stack on top of the National Intelligence Grid
(NATGRID) to analyse patterns, retrieve data and act as a tool to detect
cross-state terror financing. The digital policing stack consists of
several constituent elements that layer on top of one another to move
policing from reactive investigation toward predictive, continuous, and
data-driven governance.

We note that at each layer, there is a unique risk that is specific to
that layer, and there have been [well-documented
harms](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/algorithmic-policing-in-india-the-case-for-a-governing-framework)
emerging from this set-up. However, much of AI governance—especially
in the field of predictive policing—assumes that predominant risk
emerges from the algorithm itself despite, somewhat counterintuitively,
recognising that a major cause for algorithmic harm is the datasets that
exist within it. This is a category error. Legal risk is not something
that switches on only once an algorithm is deployed; it accrues
progressively, at every point where the state exercises a choice over
information. To see why, it helps to ask a simple question at each
layer: what has the state _done_ to the data, and does that act carry
independent legal significance?

**Data collection determines what information exists.** Before any
classification or analysis happens, the state has already made a
threshold decision about which facts about a citizen enter the record at
all—biometric markers, forensic samples, CCTV footage, complaint
histories. This is not a neutral act of "gathering." Under the
_Puttaswamy_ framework, collection itself engages the right to privacy,
because the very existence of a record about a person, retrievable and
re-usable indefinitely, is a distinct harm from whatever use is
eventually made of it. A CCTNS entry sits dormant for years, but its
creation already forecloses a certain kind of anonymity.

**Classification determines what that information means.** For instance,
the Tamil Nadu Police recently adopted the Sexual Offender Profiling,
Evaluation, Classification, Tracking, Risk Assessment and Unified
Monitoring System
("[SPECTRUM](https://www.ndtv.com/india-news/tamil-nadu-introduces-spectrum-to-classify-and-monitor-sexual-offenders-11704147)"),
which assigns sexual offenders to colour-coded risk categories based on
their perceived likelihood of reoffending. SPECTRUM does not add new
facts about a person; it adds a _judgment_ about the facts already
collected—Red, Amber, or Green. That judgment then travels with the
underlying record and conditions how every downstream system treats that
person, independent of whether the classification was accurate,
contestable, or even disclosed. This is where the state moves from
record-keeper to adjudicator, quietly, without the due process
architecture that ordinarily accompanies an adjudicative act.
Classification is a legally consequential exercise precisely because it
is sticky: once a person is tagged, the tag does the work that used to
require a fresh determination each time.

**Interoperability determines who can access and use it.** A record
confined to a single database, however invasive its contents, is bounded
by the purpose for which it was collected. The moment it becomes
interoperable—as NATGRID's federated-access model or the
Inter-operable Criminal Justice System are [designed to
allow](https://www.mha.gov.in/en/commoncontent/inter-operable-criminal-justice-system-icjs)—that
boundary dissolves. Purpose limitation, one of the DPDP Act's
foundational principles, is not violated by any single act of
collection; it is violated by the infrastructural decision to make data
legible and queryable across agencies that had no role in collecting it.
Interoperability is, in effect, a standing authorisation for future uses
that were never contemplated at the point of consent (to the extent
consent was sought at all, given the state's [exemptions under the Act
for law-enforcement
purposes](https://everythingtmt.substack.com/p/where-the-dpdp-regime-holds-and-where)).

**Decision-support determines how it shapes policing**. This is the
layer where classification and interoperability convert into operational
consequence—a patrol pattern, a surveillance frequency, a threshold
for "caution" in an officer's interaction with a specific individual.
South Zone Inspector General Vijayendra Bidari Bidari's own
[description](https://www.ndtv.com/india-news/tamil-nadu-introduces-spectrum-to-classify-and-monitor-sexual-offenders-11704147)
of SPECTRUM makes this explicit: the system does not merely store a
categorisation; it dictates a procedure. That is a rule with the
practical force of law, generated and applied without legislative
authorisation, judicial oversight, or a codified standard of review.

**Predictive AI** determines what future inferences the State may draw
from it, and it does so through choices that are themselves legally
significant, not merely inherited from upstream layers. For instance,
the Delhi Police uses the Crime Mapping, Analytics and Predictive System
([CMAPS](https://www.hindustantimes.com/delhi/delhi-police-is-using-precrime-data-analysis-to-send-its-men-to-likely-trouble-spots/story-hZcCRyWMVoNSsRhnBNgOHI.html))
to identify potential hotspots of crimes by plotting live distress calls
and historical crime data onto a geospatial map to identify hotspots,
allowing authorities to strategically deploy patrolling resources. A
model like CMAPS is not a passive conduit for CCTNS; it makes
independent design decisions that carry their own legal weight. Which
features are engineered into the geospatial prediction, prior arrests,
complaint density, a neighbourhood's historical classification scores,
is a choice that determines whose past becomes a template for future
deployment. Feature selection is where a classification made under
SPECTRUM years earlier gets converted into a live variable weighted
against a citizen who may never have been told a "Red" tag existed, let
alone that it now feeds a hotspot algorithm patrolling their street.
Similarly, Tamil Nadu's FRS makes a threshold decision every time it
runs—the confidence score above which a match is treated as
actionable. That threshold is the line between an investigative lead and
a false accusation, set by an engineer or vendor with no public,
contestable standard governing where it should sit. These are not "AI
risks" in the generic sense the discourse usually means (bias,
hallucination, opacity); they are specific legal choices—about
evidentiary weight, about whose data trains the model, about what counts
as a sufficient match—made inside the model architecture itself, and
they deserve to be treated as such rather than folded into a vague
complaint about "algorithmic bias."

So, what would a solution look like? The answer lies in recognising that
every layer of the digital policing stack discussed above is an
independent exercise of state power over information, carrying its own
legal significance irrespective of what happens at subsequent stages. By
the time an AI system recommends a patrol route, flags an individual, or
predicts a crime hotspot, it is operating on a series of choices the
State has already made—what information was collected, how it was
classified, who it was shared with, and how it was operationalised. AI
is therefore not an independent or objective decision-maker; it is the
culmination of an existing socio-technical system.

This is where the
[sociotechnical](https://datasociety.net/research-library/a-sociotechnical-approach-to-ai-policy/)
framework becomes indispensable, because it explains why isolating the
algorithm for scrutiny will always produce an incomplete account of
harm. A sociotechnical reading insists that the "social" and the
"technical" cannot be prised apart—the outcome of a predictive
policing tool is never a property of the model alone, but of the model's
interaction with the institutions, workflows, and human judgment
surrounding it. Applied to the digital policing stack, this means that
CMAPS does not "cause" [over-policing of a
locality](https://vidhilegalpolicy.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/The-Use-of-Facial-Recognition-Technology-for-Policing-in-Delhi-compressed.pdf)
in the way a faulty component causes a machine to fail. It causes
over-policing _in combination with_ how classification labels were
assigned upstream, how interoperability rules routed that data to a
particular unit, and how an officer on the ground chooses to exercise
the discretion that decision-support layers structure but do not
eliminate. Auditing the algorithm's statistical validity, while
necessary, answers only the narrowest of the questions a court or
regulator should be asking, and cannot tell you which layer caused the
final harm.

This is also precisely why the
[techno-legal](https://psa.gov.in/CMS/web/sites/default/files/publication/AI-WP_TechnoLegal.pdf)
approach acts as a natural governance response. Rather than treating
regulation as something imposed externally once an AI system is
deployed, it argues that legal, technical and institutional safeguards
should be embedded throughout the lifecycle of a system. Applied to the
digital policing stack, this means that governance cannot begin at the
predictive AI layer because legally significant choices have already
been made long before an algorithm is introduced. Instead, safeguards
must accompany each transformation of information within the stack:
robust standards governing data collection, transparent and contestable
criteria for systems such as SPECTRUM, clear limits on interoperability
between databases, documented rules governing decision-support systems,
and continuous oversight of predictive models after deployment.

This model's lifecycle framework is useful here because it refuses to
let the model stage collapse into a single monolithic "deployment"
moment. Applied to CMAPS or FRS, this means that a model which performs
well during testing may nevertheless generate harmful outcomes once
deployed in changing operational environments. A techno-legal response,
consistent with the white paper's own proposed controls, would mandate
model-specific instruments at each of these sub-stages: red-teaming and
bias scoring before a model like FRS is approved for deployment, and
runtime monitoring with defined confidence thresholds once it's live,
rather than a single upfront certification that assumes a model's
behaviour is fixed at the point of training.

Naturally, this does not mean every layer requires the same form of
regulation. Data collection raises questions of necessity,
proportionality and minimisation. Classification raises questions of
transparency, contestability and procedural fairness. Interoperability
raises concerns around purpose limitation and access controls.
Predictive AI introduces distinct concerns of explainability, validation
and accountability. The point is not that every stage should be governed
identically, but that every stage should be governed because every stage
involves an independent exercise of state power over information.

Interestingly, this understanding is not entirely foreign to Indian law.
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, already proceeds on the
intuition that governance obligations do not arise only at the point
where data is ultimately used, but attach throughout its lifecycle.
Obligations relating to notice, purpose limitation, data minimisation,
accuracy, security safeguards, retention and erasure recognise that
legal significance accompanies information as it is collected,
processed, stored and ultimately deleted, rather than materialising only
when a consequential decision is made. Although the Act creates
significant exemptions for law-enforcement processing, its underlying
governance philosophy remains instructive. It reflects an appreciation
that the legal risks associated with information are cumulative,
emerging not from a single act of processing but from a series of
decisions about how information is handled over time. In many ways, this
lifecycle-oriented approach mirrors the logic underlying the digital
policing stack itself.

Most of our discussion has focused on how human error plays a huge role
in how the digital policing stack is influenced by human biases. This
means that a techno-legal approach, as discussed, cannot operate in
isolation, and requires a socio-technical perspective of governance as
well. This means that governance should extend to the state officials
who interact with decision-support tools: training that emphasises the
limitations of algorithmic outputs, documented reasoning when
recommendations are followed or overridden, and periodic institutional
review to identify patterns of over-reliance or misuse. Eliminating
discretion altogether is not the way forward—it has been
[recognized](<https://www.policefoundationindia.org/images/resources/pdf/MOHANTY_-ARREST_DISCREION_BEHAVIOR_POLICE_IN_INDIA_30_JULY_(1).pdf>)
as an imperative part of law-enforcement procedure.

The challenge, therefore, is to shift the object of governance away from
the algorithm alone and towards the information lifecycle that makes
algorithmic policing possible. Each stage of the digital policing stack
represents an independent exercise of state power over information and
therefore warrants its own set of legal, technical and institutional
safeguards. Governance should accompany information throughout its
lifecycle, from collection and classification to interoperability,
operational use and predictive analysis, if India's digital policing
infrastructure is to remain both technologically capable and
constitutionally legitimate.
