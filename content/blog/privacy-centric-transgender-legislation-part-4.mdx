## Conclusion

The state's relationship with the transgender community has, across
centuries and legislative iterations, been defined not by protection but
by legibility. From the registers of eunuchs maintained under the CT Act
to the transgender certificates issued under the 2019 Act, and now to
the medical board examinations and automatic surgical disclosures
mandated by the 2026 Amendment, the Indian state has consistently
premised its engagement with transgender persons on a single condition:
that they first make themselves visible to the apparatus of governance.
The language has changed from criminal control to welfare protection,
but the underlying architecture has not.

This piece has argued that such an architecture is not merely
inadequate, but fundamentally incompatible with the constitutional
guarantees affirmed in NALSA and Puttuswamy. The right to
self-determination, the inviolability of bodily autonomy, and the
protection of SOGI as sensitive personal data are binding constitutional
commitments that the 2019 Act only partially honoured, and that the 2026
Amendment has moved decisively to shut down. The certification
mechanism, subjected to the Puttuswamy test, fails on
proportionality—it imposes severe and irreversible privacy costs in
pursuit of an anti-discrimination objective that, by its very nature,
does not require the state to know who a person is, only that they were
treated adversely for who they appeared to be.

Legislation that enables self-identification within existing
documentation systems, prohibits non-consensual disclosure of prior
gender identity, and imposes strict data minimisation on welfare-linked
data collection does not weaken the protective function of law. It
strengthens it by extending protection to all those who face
discrimination on the basis of perceived gender nonconformity,
regardless of whether the state has certified their identity.

What India requires, therefore, is not a better version of the existing
architecture, but a fundamentally different one—legislation that
treats SOGI as opaque by design and transparent by choice, that
decouples anti-discrimination protection from identity certification
entirely, and that prevents the emergence of the "360-degree"
surveillance profile that the current trajectory of law and data
governance makes increasingly possible. The transgender community has
waited long enough for the state to honour the constitutional promise
made in NALSA. That promise was never about creating a new registry—it
was about recognising that no person should have to prove who they are
in order to be protected from harm for who they appear to be.
