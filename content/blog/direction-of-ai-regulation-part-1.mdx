Global perspectives on Artificial Intelligence ("AI") Regulation have
varied—naturally, given the wide and novel applications of the
technology. However, disparities in approaches make it extremely
difficult to keep track of how regulations are evolving, and how
different players in the AI market can expect to stay compliant with
regulations and best practices moving forward. This piece looks to
simplify (as much as possible) 3 things: (1) what we mean by AI, and
what the components of AI Regulation are; (2) what the broad, principled
approaches are, and which countries do what, and; (3) what scholars
propose India must do to create a future-proof, rights-centric AI
framework.

Naturally, this means that we will take a cross-jurisdictional approach,
examining the USA, the European Union, China, and India.

## What _Is_ AI?

This question is deceptively simple. The manner in which AI is defined
often determines the manner of regulation set to be followed. That is,
if you define AI as a tool, you regulate the user. If you define it as a
product, you regulate the manufacturer. If you define it as a
"frontier" threat, you regulate the science itself. However, broadly,
the OECD's
[definition](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/03/explanatory-memorandum-on-the-updated-oecd-definition-of-an-ai-system_3c815e51/623da898-en.pdf)
of AI proves to be quite a helpful starting point:

> _An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or
> implicit objectives, infers, from the input it
> receives, how to generate outputs such as
> predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence
> physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their
> levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment._
> (emphasis added)

Put (extremely) simply, the AI system is fed vast volumes of data, and
is trained to recognize patterns in such data—known as the "training
data." This constitutes the "training" segment of the [AI
lifecycle](https://www.datascience-pm.com/ai-lifecycle/). Following this
the AI is tested and tweaked, and then deployed. Following deployment,
new learning is typically limited for this specific model. To understand
how AI training, machine learning, neural networks etc. work in some
detail, refer
[here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=m8o2GrbR3d8&t=124s).

**The challenges faced in regulating AI stem from the core elements
outlined in the definition.**

**_First_**, the primary _source_ of AI's decision making is the input
it is trained on, which influences (sometimes negatively) the manner in
which AI reacts to situations. A
[report](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/10/01/1124621/openai-india-caste-bias/)
in October 2025, for instance, disclosed that ChatGPT's decision making,
and outputs were steeped in caste-bias—likely resulting from the
nature of training data fed. The long-term harms of these biases are
reinforcement of caste-based stereotypes, especially since India has
emerged as ChatGPT's [larger
user-base](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-news-updates/india-surpasses-us-as-largest-chatgpt-user-base-report-finds/#:~:text=June%203%2C%202025-,India%20Surpasses%20US%20as%20Largest%20ChatGPT%20User%20Base%2C%20Report%20Finds,a%20data%20centre%20in%20India.).
Similar concerns of bias
[emerge](https://dig.watch/updates/musks-influence-puts-grok-at-the-centre-of-ai-bias-debate)
with respect to Grok—the AI agent associated with X (formerly
Twitter).

Governing the training data, however, requires extensive disclosures by
AI developers—something they are unlikely to be willing to comply with
due to
[concerns](https://academic.oup.com/jiplp/article/20/3/182/7922541)
regarding protecting trade secrets, intellectual property, or even
market dominance.

**_Second_**, the question of what type of decision/output is generated
by an AI system is based on the objectives set or the specific
prompt/request by the user. The extent to which the AI system can
autonomously act plays a huge role on how liability may be attributed
through [existing legal
frameworks](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf).

The most commonly used AI systems—Generative AI systems—operate with
minimal autonomy, making attribution of liability _relatively_ less
complex. For instance, recently, several users on X were able to use
Grok to generate [sexually explicit
images](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/grok-says-safeguard-lapses-led-images-minors-minimal-clothing-x-2026-01-02/)
of women, including minors—even morphing publicly available
photographs of the victims. While Grok typically has had guardrails in
place to prevent such usage, an apparent lapse had occurred. The lapse
notwithstanding, it is clear that Grok had exercised minimal autonomy
over the task, and the images were a result of direct
prompts—liability over which can be accurately identified using
technologically neutral laws.

However, where the system is _Agentic_ – operating with more autonomy,
to fulfil a set task/objective, such as self-driving cars – identifying
liability of harms becomes more complex, and is likely to require a deep
dive on what exactly caused the harm, whether it was because of the
training data, a hardware issue, or some externality. Determination
becomes time-consuming and resource-intensive.

**_Third_,** academics point out that AI systems have become "black
boxes," systems which can be understood in terms of their input and
output, but whose internal workings are a mystery to (the average)
observers. Generally, this is because the manner in which machines learn
involves [internalization of
data](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/assets/articlePDFs/v31/The-Artificial-Intelligence-Black-Box-and-the-Failure-of-Intent-and-Causation-Yavar-Bathaee.pdf#page=10.41)
that is not easily understood, much less audited, by humans.
Consequentially, this opacity breeds difficult-to-regulate risks, and
requires a nuanced solution which balances excessive disclosures with
transparency.

These challenges are by no means exclusive, and often interact and
compound the impact of one another. For instance, since AI systems are
often black boxes, understanding biases in training data becomes
impossible. Or, since autonomy impacts the manner of decision making,
and its relationship with the user, it becomes difficult to determine
where liability of the developer ends and where that of the user begins.
Essentially, these challenges are interlinked: biased data, autonomy,
and opacity together shape the core regulatory problem of AI.

As a result, contemporary regulatory efforts are structured around
managing risks arising from biased training data, varying levels of
autonomy, and the opacity of AI decision-making, rather than attempting
comprehensive ex ante regulation.
