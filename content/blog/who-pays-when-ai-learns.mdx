The Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade ("DPIIT") on
08.12.2025 published Part I of the working paper by the Committee on
Generative Artificial Intelligence and Copyright ("the Committee") –
focused on the "One Nation One License One Payment" Model. The Committee
was tasked with answering the following questions:

1.  How does Generative AI ("AI") interact with copyright law in India?

2.  Whether the current legal framework is adequate to address
    challenges emerging from these interactions? Are any amendments
    needed?

The Committee therefore identified two major areas of the intersection
of AI and copyright law: (a) Use of copyrighted works as training data,
usually without permission—dealt with by the present working paper,
and; (b) copyright in AI-generated outputs, which will be addressed in
Part II of the working paper. Interestingly, in Part I, the Committee
does not draw a formal or doctrinal distinction between "training" and
"pre-training." It deliberately treats them as part of a single,
continuous process of AI training for copyright analysis.

India's Current AI-Copyright Status Quo

The paper discusses in some detail what India's approach to AI
regulation is – first examining the India's National AI Strategy &
IndiaAI Mission. The following are the critical points raised:

1.  The working paper notes that the National Strategy on AI, released
    by NITI Aayog in 2018 under the theme #AIforAll, envisioned AI as a
    transformational tool across sectors and highlighted the need to
    strengthen India's IP ecosystem to support AI innovation.

2.  Building on this, the IndiaAI Mission, approved in 2024 with an
    investment of over ₹10,300 crore, aims to create a unified,
    sovereign AI ecosystem, and as a result of such large-scale
    investment, thousands of GPUs are being deployed at subsidised
    rates, multiple ministries are being integrated into a national
    dataset-sharing framework, new labs and fellowships are being rolled
    out across educational institutions, and indigenous LLM efforts
    (such as those by Sarvam AI, Gnani AI, and others) are already being
    funded.

3.  Parallelly, India's creative economy amounts to \~4.3% of GDP,
    employs millions (including informal folk sectors), and is projected
    to grow at a CAGR of 13% between 2021 and 2026 making it \$80
    billion USD economy.

4.  Further, a report by the International Council for Research on
    International Economic Relations (ICRIER) estimates that India's
    informal music industry alone employs over 1.4 crore people.

The working paper, therefore, acknowledges the importance of a balanced
approach, and looks to understand various models, and how stakeholders
from both the AI developers' side and the creators' side have reacted to
the same.

Stakeholder Consultations

The Committee consulted a variety of stakeholders (listed out in
Annexure A), specifically:

- AI developers, tech companies

- Publishers, music & film industries

- News associations

- Collecting societies

- Legal experts

Unsurprisingly, the Committee received polarised views from the Tech
Industry and Content Industry, as is represented in the table below:

| Aspect                            | Tech / AI Industry View                                                                                    | Content Industry View                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Preferred regulatory approach** | Blanket Text and Data Mining (TDM) exception                                                               | Voluntary licensing only                                                  |
| **Scope of use**                  | Unrestricted AI training on copyrighted works where access is lawful                                       | Use of copyrighted works only with permission/licence                     |
| **Licensing burden**              | Minimal or no licensing obligations                                                                        | Licensing seen as essential to protect creator interests                  |
| **Position on opt-out**           | Some stakeholders supported opt-out rights for copyright holders                                           | Opt-out systems strongly opposed                                          |
| **View on data requirements**     | AI training requires very large and diverse datasets                                                       | Training involves entire works, not limited or insubstantial use          |
| **Transaction costs**             | Licensing is slow, fragmented, and high-cost, especially across millions of works                          | Licensing allows creators to negotiate value and terms                    |
| **Impact on startups/MSMEs**      | Complex negotiations are unworkable for startups and small players                                         | Voluntary licensing does not justify erosion of copyright protection      |
| **Impact on creators**            | TDM exception seen as necessary to sustain innovation                                                      | TDM exception would strip creators of control and bargaining power        |

**What the Law Says**

The Committee further analysed the existing legal framework, and how it
addresses copyright concerns with respect to AI. Broadly, it observes
that there is a definite vacuum that needs rectification, and
subsequently analyses various models to address these issues.

1.  The Copyright Act 1957 ("**the Act**") was designed for conventional
    modes of use and dissemination of works rather than large-scale
    computational ingestion of content. That is, the Act never
    envisioned the nature of "usage" of copyrighted materials that forms
    the basis of AI training.

2.  Section 14 of the Act provides a comprehensive set of rights that
    are exclusive to the copyright owner with respect to such
    copyrighted works, which broadly include the right to storage,
    adaptation, copying, and reproduction. Section 52 of the Copyright
    Act provides for situations where explicit authorization is not
    provided, but the manner of work does not constitute infringement,
    referred to as "fair use" or "fair dealing." Fundamentally, if the
    rights under Section 14 are used by an individual not authorised to
    do so (whether under Section 14 or 52), it is considered as
    infringement of copyright.

3.  The Committee observed that the training lifecycle breached Section
    14 in various ways – as may be understood from the figure below:

| Stage Number | AI Training Stage                 | What Happens                                                                                                                                                                                       | Legal Concern u/s 14                                       |
| ------------ | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1            | Data Collection (Ingestion stage) | AI developers collect vast quantities of data, often through web scraping, bulk downloads, or aggregation of datasets containing books, news articles, images, music, and other copyrighted works. | Copying of copyrighted works                               |
| 2            | Data Storage                      | Collected works are stored on servers, cloud infrastructure, or data centres for processing and training.                                                                                          | Storage amounts to reproduction                            |
| 3            | Dataset Preparation               | Raw content is cleaned, converted into machine-readable formats, filtered, and organised into training datasets.                                                                                   | Reproduction and adaptation (in a different material form) |
| 4            | Model Training                    | The AI model is trained by repeatedly exposing it to training data, during which multiple temporary or intermediate copies of works are generated.                                                 | Repeated and intentional copying                           |
| 5            | Learned Representations           | The model retains learned representations derived from training data, enabling it to generate outputs. Instances of reproduction of training data have emerged.                                    | Risk of memorisation and reproduction                      |

4.  The Committee further observed that there existed "fair use"
    provision under Section 52 either for Text and Data Mining
    ("**TDM**"), which is the method used by AI developers to procure
    massive datasets. Legislations across the world have recognised TDM
    as a valid exception to copyright laws, forming a part of the fair
    use/fair dealing doctrine. In India, however, Section 52(1)(a) is
    extremely narrow in scope, and allows fair dealing only for:

    a. private/personal use (including research)

    b. criticism/review

    c. reporting of current events

But AI training is commercial, large-scale, profit-oriented, so the
exceptions so provided are unlikely to fit.

5.  AI developers have argued, nonetheless, that AI training merely
    extracts "information" or "statistical patterns" rather than
    expressive content, which would put it outside the ambit of
    copyright infringement. However, the Working Paper treats such
    claims with caution, noting that courts and commentators
    increasingly recognise that the training process may internalise
    expressive elements of works, particularly where models are capable
    of memorising and reproducing substantial portions of training data.

6.  The legal concerns regarding copying (see Table 2) cannot be cleanly
    enforced either, since temporary and intermediate copies are made
    during AI training, and Indian law has no guidance regarding
    enforcement. Developers are unclear if they are infringing rights,
    and rightsholders are unclear about their rights.

7.  **\*ANI v. OpenAI **--\* Delhi HC is considering whether the storage
    and use of copyrighted news content for AI training amounts to
    infringement and whether fair dealing can apply.

**Models Analysed**

The Committee picked out five global models, and analysed their
applicability to India.

**Voluntary Licensing Model (USA)**

Here, AI developers negotiate license with copyright holders before
using copyrighted works for AI training. Scope of these licenses is
determined through private contractual arrangements, with no statutory
intervention beyond ordinary copyright enforcement.

The Committee noted that while this is extremely sound in theory, it
poses a few problems:

1.  Huge operating costs for licensing;

2.  Asymmetric bargaining power between negotiating parties;

3.  Impracticality of licensing millions of works individually.

*Quick note: *Apart from this, note that the licensing model of the USA
is supplemented by an extremely flexible fair use model, which allows
TDM so long as the work is "sufficiently transformative." Essentially,
an extremely open, consumer centric market protects bargaining power,
but at the same time, as long as the works are "transformed," the AI
developer need not seek a separate license. Neither is India's market is
as open or consumer-centric as the US, nor is the fair-use exceptions
sufficiently wide, making this model impractical.

**Collective and Extended Collective Licensing Model [CL/ECL] (Nordic
Belt)**

ECL allows a recognised collective management organisation (CMO)—the
European equivalent of copyright societies—to license not only its
members' works, but also non-members' works within the same category,
unless those rightsholders explicitly opt out. Royalties are collected
centrally and distributed to both members and non-members.

The Committee noted that while this is a rights-cum-economic centric
approach, it requires an institutional set-up that is absent in India:

1.  Mature CMOs/copyright societies;

2.  Close to universal representation of rightsholders in such CMOs;

3.  Transparent governance models;

4.  CMOs are required to actively search for any author or owners who
    cannot be identified or

5.  located at the time when the licence for use is granted.

*Quick note: *The Committee also observes that CL and ECL solve two
major issues, i.e., consent and remuneration from the copyright owners'
perspective, and reduce transaction costs from the AI Developer
perspective. However, it determined that a major challenge is that the
collective bargaining position may be leveraged for an excessive royalty
rate. This feels like a rather poor argument to make, since one of the
core objectives of the CMOs is to ensure collective bargaining, since
there is already an imbalance between rightsholders and AI developers.
However, the associated challenge of incumbent developers benefiting
from this system very much holds good.

**Statutory Licensing (India)**

Here, instead of work-specific or owner-specific licenses, users are
required to pay a pre-determined royalty calculated on government or
court fixed rates, as compensation to the rightsholders. Statutory
licensing also reduces transaction costs and creates a predictable
environment for licensees of works. While this model takes away the
power of the copyright owners to refuse licensing or negotiate a fee, it
guarantees them fair compensation.

The Committee notes that this model reduces transaction costs, improves
predictability, and ensures that copyright owners receive a fair royalty
as a trade-off for the power of the copyright owners to refuse licensing
or negotiate a fee. However, it does have a few issues in the context of
Indian law:

1.  Statutory licensing under Indian copyright law is not automatic.
    Existing statutory and compulsory licences (for example under
    Sections 31, 31A, and 31D of the Copyright Act) require active
    procedural engagement with regulatory authorities, since AI training
    is not a one-time act but a continuous, iterative activity, repeated
    procedural compliance is impractical;

2.  Determining licensing costs assumes that the user can identify the
    categories of the work, the volume and nature of use can be
    reasonably specified, and compliance can be monitored against
    declared parameters. However, since AI training uses constantly
    evolving technologies, this would mean constant reporting and
    updates to the authorities—incompatible with the dynamic and
    automated nature of AI development.

**Blanket TDM Exceptions**

This refers to unrestricted, royalty-free use of copyrighted material
for TDM and AI training purposes, without requiring individual licenses
or permissions, so long as the user has lawful access to the material.
This specific model was proposed by the tech community and was strongly
rejected by the Committee, citing the following grounds:

1.  Copyright is built on the idea that creators are entitled to control
    and economically exploit their works. A blanket TDM exception
    removes both elements at once. By allowing AI developers to freely
    use copyrighted works for training, the exception effectively
    converts creative labour into a free input for a commercial
    industry, and effectively reducing the incentive to create. Further,
    if creators cannot control use or remuneration, they have lost the
    power to exploit their works.

2.  India's informal creative economy lacks formal representation, legal
    knowledge or even membership in CMOs/societies. A blanket TDM
    exception would expose these creators' works to large-scale
    commercial use without any possibility of compensation or redress,
    disproportionately harming those already least protected.

3.  Once the data is used for training, it is irreversible. The
    consequences are functionally permanent, and the creators no longer
    have the capacity to exercise practical control over the
    exploitation of their works. This is exacerbated by the capacity of
    AI models to memorise or reproduce works.

**TDM Exceptions with Opt-Out (EU)**

Here, AI developers may use copyrighted material only if rightsholder(s)
have not explicitly denied (opted-out of) such use. In practice, the
European Union's** Copyright in the Digital Single Market
Directive** ("**CDSMD**") allows two exceptions: (i) for scientific
research (by universities, research bodies, etc.), or; (ii) for
commercial purposes where the rightsholders have not opted out from data
collection.

The Committee notes that this is quite burdensome to the copyright
owner, and lacks mechanisms to prevent large-scale or permanent harms:

1.  The Committee criticises the reversal of burden: instead of AI
    developers proving permission, creators must police the use of their
    own works. This is especially challenging for smaller creators, who
    do not know when or how their works are being scraped, nor do they
    have the technical means to signal opt-out effectively. As a result,
    opt-out protections would primarily benefit large publishers, not
    individual creators.

2.  Further, opt-out cannot help control downstream processing, reuse of
    derived datasets or training on retained data – i.e., once content
    enters the training pipeline, opt-out operates too late. Metadata
    stripping and data aggregation further weaken the effectiveness of
    such reservations.

3.  Further, if opt-outs are used excessively, then datasets may become
    smaller, less diverse or low quality, causing degrading model
    quality, increase bias and hallucinations, and ultimately undermine
    the stated innovation benefits of the TDM exception itself. Further,
    to check whether opt-outs have been followed, the developer should
    be subject to transparency requirements, which the Committee frames
    as being burdensome, and anti-innovation.

*Quick note: *The issues cited by the Committee are valid, but lack
nuance. Please note:

1.  The Committee treats this burden-shifting as a *necessary
    feature* of opt-out, rather than a **design choice**. It does not
    distinguish between:

    a. decentralised opt-out (robots.txt, metadata flags, contractual
    notices), and

    b. centralised, state-backed opt-out mechanisms with upstream
    enforcement.

As a result, the critique collapses all opt-out models into their
weakest existing form, without considering whether institutional
mediation between the two proposed models could rebalance burdens back
onto developers.

2.  The Committee implicitly assumes that all opt-out operates ex post,
    after data ingestion. It therefore treats irreversibility as a
    reason to reject opt-out entirely, rather than as an argument for
    ex-ante enforcement.

3.  The threat of data shrinkage is inevitable if the Committee wishes
    to respect the consent of copyright owners. In fact, the premise of
    this paper itself is how copyright owners can better exercise their
    rights while allowing for AI development – which should make the
    primary consideration how developers/government can incentivise
    copyright owners to give permission (sufficient remuneration or
    granular consent to usage could prove to be good incentives).

4.  Fundamentally, the Committee frames transparency almost exclusively
    as an **innovation tax**, rather than as a **governance
    necessity, **especially given the power imbalance and market
    dominance at play. Other highly innovative sectors (finance,
    telecom, pharmaceuticals) routinely accept transparency and
    auditability as baseline conditions**. The Committee does not fully
    justify why AI training should be treated as uniquely fragile to
    compliance obligations.**

**Proposed Model**

Summarily, the Committee recognises the following elements as necessary
for regulating the domain:

1.  Availability of lawfully accessed content for training to AI
    Developers as a matter of right without the need for negotiations,
    ensuring high-quality AI development with mitigated risk of AI bias

2.  Fair compensation to copyright holders

3.  Rate setting via a quick, transparent process

4.  Preserving the ability of affected parties to challenge/ask for
    review of rates established before a judicial forum

5.  Facilitates both big players, start-ups and individuals in the AI
    tech as well as creative industry, creating no barriers to entry

6.  Upholds the basic principles of copyright and rewards human
    creativity

7.  Mitigates risk of litigation and disputes.

**Core Premise: Mandatory Access with Compensatory Protection**

The foundational element of the Committee's proposed model is the
separation of access from control. The model proceeds on the assumption
that large-scale AI training requires unrestricted access to diverse
datasets and that individual veto rights over training data would make
such access unworkable. Accordingly, the Committee proposes that AI
developers should be legally permitted to use all lawfully accessible
copyrighted works for training purposes as a matter of right. In
exchange, copyright owners are no longer granted the ability to refuse
such use, but are instead protected through a guaranteed statutory right
to remuneration.

**Removal of Consent and Opt-Out Mechanisms**

The Committee takes the view that consent-based or opt-out mechanisms
would fragment training datasets, increase compliance complexity, and
undermine innovation, particularly for startups and domestic AI
developers. So, according to the model proposed, once a work is lawfully
accessible, it is automatically available for AI training under the
proposed framework, without any requirement for express permission or
reservation of rights by the copyright owner. By replacing negotiated
licences with a uniform statutory mechanism, the Committee looks to
prevent a scenario where only well-resourced, established AI developers
can afford to comply with complex licensing regimes**.**

The Committee also looks to reduce dataset fragmentation, bias and
hallucination (which were argued to be consequences of an opt-out
mechanism reducing the quantity and quality of datasets). That is, by
ensuring universal access to lawfully available content, the model seeks
to preserve dataset diversity and prevent AI systems from being trained
on narrow or low-quality data.

**Statutory Remuneration**

Since the copyright owner has lost a great degree of control over the
works, the Committee introduces a statutory remuneration right for
copyright holders. This right operates independently of consent and
ensures that creators receive compensation whenever their works are used
for AI training. Unlike voluntary licensing, remuneration is not
determined through private negotiation but through government-determined
royalty rates, which are intended to reflect the scale and economic
value of AI training activities.

*Calculation:* The model proposes the creation of
a **government-appointed rate-setting body to determine applicable
royalty rates**. This mechanism is expected to consider factors such as
the category of works used, the scale of AI training, and the commercial
benefits derived by AI developers.

*Distribution:* The model purports a "one licence, one payment" approach
through a single, central, non-profit body—proposed as the Copyright
Royalties Collective for AI Training ("**CRCAT**"). AI developers would
make payments under a single blanket licence, while CRCAT would be
responsible for collecting, managing, and distributing royalties to
copyright societies, collective management organisations, and individual
rightsholders, including non-members who register their works. Royalties
are pooled by the CRCAT into different categories (A/V, text, music,
etc.), and **all rightsholders within a category share in the pool,
regardless of whether their specific work can be shown to have been used
in training.**

*Membership: *The Committee proposes that the CRCAT should distribute
royalties not only to members of recognised copyright societies, but
also to non-member rightsholders, provided they register their works
with the collective.

**Reduced Transparency Obligations**

To ensure that innovation is protected, the Committee proposed reduced
transparency obligations on AI developers. Rather than requiring
disclosure of training datasets, verification of individual permissions,
or continuous reporting of data sources, compliance is largely limited
to participation in the collective licensing system and payment of
royalties. That is, AI developers are expected to:

- obtain a blanket licence,

- pay royalties to a central collective body, and

- comply with general regulatory conditions,

without having to reveal dataset composition or individual works used.

**Critical Analysis**

1.  Through this model, it is clear that the Committee has taken a
    pro-innovation approach, appearing to be at the cost of the rights
    of the copyright holder. The objective of Section 14 of the Act is
    to allow the owner to control how their original work is used,
    reproduced, and monetized, and to prevent unauthorized use by others
    – forming the basis of "commercial exploitation." However, when the
    copyright owner has no control over if and how they wish to be
    remunerated, would the essence of the rights granted to them remain?
    The Committee does not convincingly establish why AI training
    warrants such a broad and permanent exception, particularly when the
    use is commercial and extractive rather than incidental or
    public-serving in nature.

Additional concerns emerge from the nature of the license itself. The
DPIIT does not clarify if the license in question exists because AI
Training constitutes a breach of copyright. Put simply, why should the
license exist? Is AI training without a license a breach of S.14 of the
Act? If so, what is the nature of such breach, and why can it not be
enforced under the existing licensing regime?

2.  On the matter of remuneration, the Committee at no point provides
    clarity on:

- How will copyright owners know whether their works have been used?

- How will the government/relevant authorities police the usage of
  these works and the payment of royalties?

These questions do not arise from the need of granularity. They are
important to clarify, however, since the Committee claimed that
transparency is an excessive burden, and yet requires the payment of
royalties, which are to be distributed on the basis of datasets used.
How will these be identified without transparency requirements is to be
clarified. Furthermore, *reduced* transparency requirements have been
proposed, not requiring any dataset disclosures, making the questions
all the more pertinent.

3.  A broader concern, actually, is that the Committee repeatedly frames
    creator-facing safeguards, like opt-outs, dataset transparency, or
    attribution of rights (moral rights) as "burdensome" or
    "anti-innovation," while treating developer convenience as a neutral
    baseline, even going so far as to critique collective bargaining as
    anti-innovative owing to the possibility of leveraging a higher
    royalty pay. By treating innovation arising from AI training as
    "uniquely fragile" to compliance obligations, the model effectively
    insulates developers from accountability mechanisms that are routine
    in other highly innovative sectors such as finance or
    telecommunications.

4.  The opt-out model was criticised for requiring awareness on part of
    the copyright owner, especially when the copyright owner is part of
    India's massive informal economy. The proposed hybrid model,
    however, does not clarify how the problem will be addressed
    post-implementation. That is, owners will still require knowledge
    regarding registration and the royalties they will have the right
    to. If awareness building is possible here, why could the opt-out
    model, which is more creator-centric, have been implemented along
    with such awareness programs?

Summarily, the proposed model prioritises innovation through compulsory
access and pooled remuneration, but in doing so, it sacrifices creator
control, transparency, and accountability in a manner that risks
entrenching market power and hollowing out the core values of copyright
law. While the model may facilitate rapid AI development, it does so by
redefining copyright in ways that are insufficiently justified, weakly
accountable, and likely to deepen existing power asymmetries.
