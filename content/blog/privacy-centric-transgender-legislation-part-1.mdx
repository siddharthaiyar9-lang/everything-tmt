## Introduction

Global discourse on protecting the rights of transgender people has been
fairly diverse in terms of defining what exactly constitutes
"protection" of rights. Nonetheless, Spade
([2009](https://www.deanspade.net/wp-content/uploads/2010/07/templefinal.pdf))
provides us with a useful summary:

> "Advocates of trans equality have primarily pursued two law reform
> interventions: anti-discrimination laws that list gender identity
> and/or expression as a protected category and hate crimes laws that
> include crimes motivated by the gender identity and/or expression of
> the victim as triggering the application of a jurisdiction's hate
> crimes statute."[^1]

However, a major consideration that is often left out in scholarly work
is the need for privacy-centric architecture within protective
legislations. The need for privacy of marginalised groups has been
observed by the UN Human Rights Council ([HRC resolution
34/7](https://ap.ohchr.org/documents/dpage_e.aspx?si=A/HRC/RES/34/7))
and the General Assembly
([A/RES/71/19](https://docs.un.org/en/a/res/71/19)), which noted that
"violations and abuses of the right to privacy in the digital age may
affect all individuals, including with particular effects on women, as
well as children and persons in vulnerable situations, or marginalised
groups."

Before we proceed with arguments of any kind, there are three things we
must qualify. First, what do we mean by "transgender"? While we will
discuss the idea of "defining" a trans person, our discussion will rely
on an inclusive definition as per the 2019 iteration of the Transgender
(Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 ("**2019 Act**"), which defines a
transgender person as a person whose gender does not match with the sex
assigned at birth.[^2]

Second, what do we mean by privacy in the context of protection of
transgender (and genderqueer) people? For this, we may also rely on the
Human Rights Committee's interpretation of Article 17 of the ICCPR in
_Coeriel et al. v. The Netherlands,_[^3] as "the sphere of a person's
life in which he or she can freely (i.e., with no
unfair/undue influence over their actions) express his or her identity,
be it by entering into relationships with others or alone." It has been
[widely
recognised](https://fpf.org/blog/out-not-outed-privacy-for-sexual-health-orientations-and-gender-identities/)
that Sexual Orientation and Gender Identity ("**SOGI**")[^4] is
sensitive personal data that should be treated with heightened
sensitivity by processors of such data. Therefore, for the purposes of
our discussion, privacy
[refers](https://www.freiheit.org/india/privacy-intrinsic-and-so-sexuality)
to a comprehensive fundamental right that includes self-identification,
control over disclosure of SOGI, and the protection of bodily
autonomy.[^5]

Third, why is the focus of our discussion limited to transgender people
when the entire LGBTQ+ community faces discriminatory practices across
society? Now, whereas it is [globally
recognised](https://www.un.org/en/fight-racism/vulnerable-groups/lgbtqi-plus)
that the LGBTQ+ community is marginalised and discriminated against,[^6]
transgender people occupy a unique position in India, since they may
simultaneously occupy a specific socio-cultural identity, such as
Hijras, Kinnars, Aravanis, Kothis, and Jogappas. This brings about a set
of unique challenges that are associated with the manner in which these
communities have been perceived by society since the colonial era. The
Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 ("**CT Act**")—a colonial
legislation—deemed the entire Hijra/transgender community as "innately
criminal" and "addicted to the systematic commission of non-bailable
offences."[^7] Despite the repeal of the CT Act in 1952, the (now
delegitimised) discrimination has perpetrated against transgender
community, and has even been institutionalised, as we will discuss
moving forward.

The Government of India has, by introducing the contentious Transgender
(Protection of Rights) Amendment Act 2026 ("**2026 Amendment**") which
amends the 2019 Act in ways that are incompatible with the fundamental
right of privacy as upheld by the Supreme Court in a slew of judgments,
as we will discuss moving forward. Through this piece, we will look into
how privacy centric architecture can be embedded in transgender-friendly
legislation.

# Defining "Transgender" Persons

The crux of the matter is establishing what constitutes a "transgender
person," since the state, in order to make governance easier, wishes to
make the subject of its governance—in this case the identity of
"transgender," legible. We borrow from Teresa De Lauretis' understanding
of gender identity as a product of social technologies such as law,
medicine, and administrative systems, as opposed to being a result of
innate biological characteristics alone.[^8] These social technologies
work together to define an "ideal subject," that the state deems to be
the subject of governance and regulation. That is; by defining the scope
of "transgender persons" the state establishes who or what falls within
the ambit of the relevant law.

The manner in which individuals' gender identities are defined at a
legal-administrative level oscillates between two methods:
self-determination and biological reductionism. The biological test
reduces gender identity to a function of biological sex—adopting a
"medico-legal" test to identify an individual's sex under the
presumption that[^9] sex and gender are congruent in nature. In the
landmark _Corbett v. Corbett_ (1970),[^10] England's High Court of
Justice established the infamous "Corbett Test," which held that the law
should adopt the chromosomal, gonadal and genital tests and if all three
are congruent, that should determine a person's sex.

Bodwal
([2022](https://repository.nls.ac.in/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1239&context=slr))[^11]
relies on De Lauretis' approach (as discussed earlier), and suggests
that social and legal institutions that construct transgender identity
on the basis of bio-medical epistemes do so for the purpose of
"legibility." That is, they make complex human identities fit—albeit
somewhat forcibly—into standardised medical categories for the purpose
of easier governance. However, he further argues that this goes beyond
mere governance and is one part of a larger approach to regulate
"deviant" populations, such as transgender people through a host of
institutions, including medicine and psychology.

The bio-medical approach, however, has been
[rejected](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gender-incongruence-and-transgender-health-in-the-icd#:~:text=The%20International%20Classification%20serves%20to,related%20to%20sexual%20health%E2%80%9D%20chapter.)
almost unequivocally at the global stage, with preference being given to
the self-determination approach instead. As the name suggests, this
approach enables individuals to self-declare their gender identity
without the imposition of any discriminatory preconditions.

The Yogyakarta Principles – internationally recognised as the
international standard for the applicability of human rights law with
respect to the LGBTQ+ community – recognises the right of
self-determination in Article 3, which states:

> "Each person's self-defined sexual orientation and gender identity is
> integral to their personality and is one of the most basic aspects of
> self-determination, dignity and freedom. No one shall be forced to
> undergo medical procedures, including sex reassignment surgery,
> sterilisation or hormonal therapy, as a requirement for legal
> recognition of their gender identity."

These principles were discussed by the Supreme Court of India in the
landmark _National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India_[^12]
("**NALSA**") where the SC recognised the importance of
self-determination as a key component of Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of
expression) and Article 21 (right to life and liberty) of the
Constitution. We may also place reliance on the Supreme Court judgment
in _Anuj Garg & Ors vs Hotel Association of India,_[^13] which
recognised self-determination as an important part of gender justice
discourse.

Internationally, in _In Re Kevin_ (Validity of Marriage of
Transsexual),[^14] the Family Court of Australia listed out[^15] some
operative characteristics that could influence legal perception of
gender, which included the sex in which he or she is brought up and the
person's attitude to it, and the person's self-perception as a man or
woman. More persuasive is the decision of the UN Human Rights Committee
which, in an extension of its seminal 1994 ruling in _Toonen v.
Australia_[^16] (Gay Rights) reiterated that the right to privacy is not
limited to sexual orientation but also covers gender identity.[^17]

However, despite near universal recognition of self-determination as a
more "transgender-friendly" approach to regulation, India, through the
2026 Amendment to the 2019 Act, has taken a step backwards, and now
limits legal recognition to historically accepted socio-cultural groups
such as _hijra_ and _kinner_, as well as intersex individuals,[^18] as
we will discuss moving forward.
