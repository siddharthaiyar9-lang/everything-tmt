## Transgender Protection of Rights Act 2019 (and the 2026 Amendment)

After the introduction of the Guidelines, the 2019 Act was introduced,
and hailed as a watershed movement in ensuring equality of transgender
people. It looked to operationalise the Supreme Court's verdict in
NALSA, but has since been heavily
[criticised](https://www.hrw.org/news/2019/07/23/india-transgender-bill-raises-rights-concerns),[^1]
and fits cleanly within our three-pronged "technology of security"
framework. The Act undertakes a paternalistic tone by framing itself as
an "Act to provide for protection of rights of transgender persons and
their welfare and for matters connected therewith and incidental
thereto."

Now, on **documentation and legibility**. The 2019 Act defined
"transgender" in an inclusive manner as "a person whose gender does not
match with the gender assigned to that person at birth and includes
trans-man or trans-woman, whether or not such person has undergone Sex
Reassignment Surgery." The definition itself is remarkably
inclusive—and fulfils the constitutional requirements under
NALSA—but was subject to operational lacunae. Whereas the first
application for a "transgender certificate"[^2] could be on the basis
of self-determined gender, the change in gender (i.e., recognition as
male or female) requires that SRS occur, and the change of gender
certificate be provided to the District Magistrate who should be
"satisfied with the correctness" of such certificate. Critics argued
that this is effectively insistence on SRS, which has been held to be
illegal and immoral in NALSA,[^3] and threaten to conflate the medical
and the legal. Insofar as the power to determine legitimacy of an
individual's gender identity rests with the state, the individual no
longer is able to freely self-determine without the influence of the
state—a violation of the privacy of the individual as determined by
the Supreme Court in Puttuswamy.

The third part on **criminalisation**. Much of the criminalisation of
the 2019 Act extends to determination of what constitutes a valid form
of kinship,[^4] and mandates their presence in rehabilitation homes
where necessary,[^5] both of which somewhat contradict the principles
outlined in Puttuswamy. However, the larger constitutional questions
emerge from the attempt at mass documentation of transgender people.

Creation of a mandatory "transgender certificate," involves mandatory
disclosure of one's gender identity. Therefore, similar to the blood
donation guidelines, we subject this to the scrutiny of the Puttuswamy
test. Whereas the aim of protecting transgender rights is a legitimate
state aim, and the introduction of the Act is legally permissible, the
primary question is whether a mandatory certificate is necessary and
proportionate to the state's aim of protecting transgender rights.

The 2019 Act does two things: (1) outlines what constitutes
discrimination of transgender people, and prohibits the same, and; (2)
enables "appropriate governments," to undertake welfare measures for
transgender people.[^6] The introduction of the transgender certificate
as a precondition for the applicability of both (1) and (2) is
disproportionate. That is, whereas it is reasonable and proportionate
for identification to be mandatory for welfare delivery, the 2019 Act
itself does not provide for any direct welfare or associated benefits,
and "welfare" under the Act is limited to an enabling clause. The actual
welfare delivery happens through subordinate legislation and executive
action entirely outside the Act's own operative provisions. This means
the certificate the Act creates serves, within the four corners of the
Act itself, only two purposes: (1) recognition of identity, and; (2)
protection from discrimination.

Now, it is neither necessary, nor proportionate to have to provide
separate identifiers for prevention of discrimination. That is to say,
the root of anti-discrimination law is the intent of the discriminator,
not the actual identity of the victim. That is, for instance, in the
case of an SC/ST Prevention of Atrocities proceeding, the caste
certificate aids in establishing that the victim was indeed a member of
the SC/ST community,[^7] but establishing the commission/abetment of a
cate-based offence requires the establishment of intent of the accused
to do so.[^8] Put simply, establishing (and thereby preventing)
discrimination of a member of a SC/ST community requires that two
elements be fulfilled: (1) the person has a valid certificate indicating
their membership within such community, and; (2) the discrimination
occurred on the basis of the caste itself. As recognised in NALSA,
gender identity is self-determined unlike caste, and therefore the
existence of a certificate is immaterial—what matters is whether the
discriminator acted on the basis of that perceived identity. The
necessity of a transgender certificate as a means to operationalise
rights granted under this Act stands in direct contravention to the
principle of self-determination, and conditions anti-discrimination
statutes on state recognition of gender identity.

The 2026 Amendment changed none of the negative elements of the 2019
Act, and instead removed some protections granted to transgender people.
The three-pronged technology of security division remains broadly
unchanged, with some modifications to documentation and legibility. Take
the Statements of Objects and Reasons of the 2026 Amendment, which
states:

> "The legislative policy was and is intended to protect only those who
> face severe social exclusion due to biological reasons for no fault of
> their own and no choice of their own [...] It is therefore
> imperative to give a precise definition for proper and definitive
> identification and protection of transgender persons to whom the
> benefits of the Act must reach. [...]care has to be taken that such
> identification cannot be extended on the basis of any acquirable
> characteristics or personal choice or claimed self-perceived identity
> of an individual."

The implications are alarming. Firstly, this rests on the assumption
that acts of discrimination and associated social exclusion of
transgender people occur solely on the basis of _verified_
biological/community identity, as opposed to perceived
identity—extrapolated from the manner in which a person acts or looks.

Second, inasmuch as a person's gender identity/sexuality is an innate
and private affair, control over such information should rest solely
with such person. The phrase "...legislative policy was and is intended
to protect only those who face severe social exclusion due to biological
reasons for no fault of their own and no choice of their own," implies
that the state is in a position to adequately determine whether an act
of discrimination or social exclusion on the basis of an individual's
gender identity (in this case their identity as transgender) was the
"fault" of the victim, and therefore whether the victim deserves
protection under the legislation. That is, the state now has the power
to determine whether an individual's gender identity is "legitimate,"
and consequently deserving of legal protection, which, as discussed
under the 2019 Act, stands in contravention of the Supreme Court's
judgment in Puttuswamy.

The 2026 Amendment first narrows the definition of "transgender," to
include those who are transgender by virtue of congenital variations in
sex characteristics, those who are born into socio-cultural communities
like _kinner, hijra, aravani_ and _jogta,_ and those who have been
forced/compelled to assume, adopt, or outwardly present a transgender
identity, going so far as to specifically exclude those who are
transgender by virtue of self-determined identities.[^9]

Next, it replaces self-identification with a medico-legal framework to
determine whether a person is transgender or not. First, Section 4(2) of
the 2019 Act, which provides for self-determination of gender, has been
omitted.[^10] Further, when a person applies for a transgender
certificate,[^11] the District Magistrate will only grant the same only
after consulting the medical board established under the 2026
Amendment.[^12] It also mandates those who undertake SRS after receiving
a certificate under Section 6 to apply for a revised certificate, the
receipt of which shall, once again be subject to the satisfaction of the
District Magistrate.

Most concerning of all is the introduction of Section 7(1A) which
mandates the medical institution that conducted such SRS shall
automatically provide the details of such receiver to the District
Magistrate.[^13] Read with the extremely state-centric data protection
regime (refer
[here](https://everythingtmt.substack.com/p/where-the-dpdp-regime-holds-and-where)),
principles of data minimisation and purpose limitation are subject to
broad exemptions for the state, and such data may be used beyond the
scope for which it was collected. Concerns[^14] have been voiced that
the state's convergence with the Brahminical family unit, and the
principle of interoperability of data could be used to create a
"360-degree" surveillance of transgender persons, causing further
stigmatisation due to hyper-visibility, and even further abuse at home
due to potential disclosures to the family.

These breaches of privacy run the risk of certain life-threatening
consequences, from outing gender identity, to exclusion of uncertified
transgender people from protection, surveillance of the certified and
policing of their behaviour, and a chilling effect on how all
transgender people express themselves.

The language of the legislation may have changed—the state now
purports to protect transgender people _from_ the harms of society—but
the State continues to render transgender identities legible through
mandatory registration, monitor them through documentation, and regulate
them through medico-legal control. In effect, the shift is merely
semantic—moving from explicit "control" to bureaucratised
"welfare"—while preserving the same surveillance-oriented
architecture. India appears to be constructing an integrated
surveillance architecture that enables the state to piece together a
near-comprehensive profile of transgender individuals across
administrative, medical, and welfare domains.

# What Privacy-Centric Legislations Could Look like

The state wants to know who is transgender, decide who can be
transgender, and determine their ideal behaviour. What changes across
150 years is only the register, the technology remains intact. This
technology is both inadequate and violative of the basic tenets of
privacy. The constitutional challenge here does not necessarily lie with
disclosure of data to the state itself—the issue is actually the
coercive architecture that renders such disclosure unavoidable for the
exercise of basic rights. A privacy centric legislation must encompass
the following considerations:

1.  Privacy of personal data must be part of the regulatory
    architecture—i.e., as part of the legislative and operational
    design. SOGI must be opaque by design and transparent by choice.

2.  The legislation should accommodate for self-identification of gender
    identity and minimise biocentric mandates—essentially respect the
    decision in NALSA.

However, we must also concede that no legislation can be operationalised
without some degree of surveillance. However, the question is not
whether the state gets the data of a transgender person or not, but
under what conditions the state receives such data. An excellent example
of a legislation that enables self-identification is Argentina's Gender
Identity Law 2012,[^16] which accommodates for self-identification as
the sole legal criteria for determination of gender identity, even
explicitly stating that "in no case" is it necessary to prove that a
person has undergone surgery for genital reassignment, hormonal
therapies, or any other psychological or medical treatment to obtain
legal recognition.[^18] Instead of creating an alternative identity
system, the Law enables recognition of the transgender identity through
self-identification within existing identity systems—even allowing for
the free modification of relevant documents.[^19] These rights are
further supported by a strict system of confidentiality—prohibiting
disclosure of the previous gender identity or amendment of recorded sex
without official authorisation.[^20]

Germany, through its Act on Self-Determination with Regard to Gender
Markers ("**SBGG**"), takes the logic of self-determination one step
forward. It recognises the need to uncouple gender assignment and the
choice of given names from assessments by third parties, extending its
focus only to civil status, not gender affirming care or associated
medical measures. Similar to Argentine law, non-consensual disclosure of
previous gender identities is strictly prohibited[^21] barring special
reasons such as criminal prosecution, security tasks, or if a
substantiated legal interest exists.

While determining the ideal components of a privacy-centric transgender
legislation, we must recognise that both Argentina and Germany are
relatively smaller countries in terms of population and therefore the
requirements for India are likely to be different in terms of scale.
Further, the existence of sociocultural communities (such as _hijras,
kinners, jogtas_ etc.) must influence the manner in which legislations
are enacted. Therefore, in sum, the legislation must prevent the
creation of a permanent, state-readable identity class—which means no
certificate-driven registry, no automatic data transfer, and no database
that lets the state assemble a "near 360-degree" profile of trans lives
by linking welfare, medical, and administrative records.

First, as opposed to creating a separate mandatory transgender
certificate—which creates a state-legible database of transgender
persons—the state must ensure that modifications to gender identity
are done within existing citizenship documentation, while taking
specific care to ensure that any information with respect to the
citizen's gender information is not disclosed without consent or
specific authorisation.

Second, the basis for modification of gender identity should be
self-identification without the requirement for any medical/biological
verification by the state. Self-identification should be enabled with
minimal state intervention. Supporters of the 2026 Amendment raise
concerns that self-identification can lead to fraudulent and repeated
modification of gender identity for the sake of availing benefits under
welfare schemes. The concern may be legitimate, but the fraud does not
emerge from gender identity itself, but from capturing benefits, and a
state that enables self-identification only does so to recognise legal
identity. Welfare schemes may hold additional criteria—such as proof
of vulnerability, income and unemployment.

Third, any data collected for welfare schemes must be scheme-specific,
minimal, and deleted once the purpose is served—i.e., subject to
strict data minimisation standards. Data Fiduciaries that collect SOGI
for the purpose of welfare schemes may be classified as Significant Data
Fiduciaries under Section 10 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023.[^22]

Fourth, automatic sharing of SOGI between entities that collect the
same—hospital, welfare boards, Aadhaar-linked systems, and
law-enforcement or family-facing databases—must be prohibited. Any
form of interoperability that enables aggregation of identity data may
facilitate the emergence of a unified, traceable dataset that could
facilitate a "360-degree" view of transgender lives. Prevention of this
is necessary and may be enforced using independent oversight, audit
trails, and strict penalties for unauthorised disclosure.
