## Surveillance, the Technology of Security and Suppression of Minority Gender Identity

## The Technology of Security

The Indian state appears to be staying firm with its tendency of
creating a form of surveillance architecture that regulates the means
and manner in which transgender individuals conduct their lives. Once
more, we will rely on Bodwal (2022),[^1] who appears to draw from
Foucauldian approaches to law and regulation,[^2] and describes the
"technology of security," as a factor in creating a more governable
society. The first pillar of this factor, **paternalism**, refers to
regulations on the grounds of protection—i.e., that the state is
acting in a "paternalistic" capacity to protect a specific group of
people through this legislation. For instance, in case of the 2019 Act
(or even the 2026 Amendment), the stated objective is to protect the
right of the oppressed transgender community. Whether the state does so
effectively is a secondary question, however.

The second pillar, **documentation and legibility,** is crucial in
determining exactly who is being governed under the law. As discussed,
when making laws, the state prefers to simply complex human identities
into standardised categories that the state can track.[^3] To do so,
the state first carries out a sorting function to determine who an
"ideal subject" is that can be governed under the legislation in
question, following which it uses an "identification apparatus" (such as
ID cards and population registers) that demands classification based on
specific pre-determined traits. That is, the state defines who a
"transgender person" is, and attempts to keep a record of them to ensure
that the benefits under the relevant laws reach them.

The third pillar, **criminalisation**, acts as the enforcement
mechanism, and operates at two levels—_implicitly_, where the social
structures traditionally attributed to the transgender community are
regulated and limited, and _explicitly,_ where transgender identity is
"controlled" directly by the state, whether through explicitly
prohibitive laws, or through "protective mechanisms," such as
"rehabilitation centres," which have long been criticised as forms of
sanitised confinement. These mechanisms have been consistently used by
the Indian state when governing transgender people, and must not be
viewed as distinct pillars operating is isolation.

## The Criminal Tribes Act 1871

The CT Act was introduced by the colonial government as a means to
provide for "registration, surveillance and control of certain criminal
tribes and eunuchs."[^4] One may note that this preambulatory clause
contains the three pillars we have discussed: first, paternalism, with
the state positioning itself as the guardian of society against
perceived threats. Second, documentation and legibility, through
mechanisms of registration and surveillance that allowed the state to
define, classify, and continuously monitor who constituted a "criminal
tribe" or a "eunuch." Third, criminalisation, as the idea of "control"
signified the use of coercive state power to manage these groups,
effectively embedding their marginalisation within a legal framework.

**Documentation and Legibility:** The CT Act established an extremely
narrow definition of eunuch—i.e., as individuals of the male sex who,
either by admission or through medical tests, are found to be
impotent.[^5] The Local Governments were required to maintain registers
of eunuchs in the area, and properties held by these eunuchs.[^6]
Importantly, if an individual was aggrieved by the addition of their
name in the list, they could appeal to the officer, but the final
decision with respect to revocation lay with the officer.

**Control:** The creation of such register made surveillance of eunuchs
and their behaviour easier to control. For instance, registered eunuchs
were barred from being "dressed or ornamented as women" in public
places,[^7] a form of regulation that prohibited one from expressing
their gender identity freely. Similarly, a registered eunuch keeping a
boy under the age of sixteen in any property in his control would be
subject to criminal punishment.[^8] This was based on a common fear in
colonial India that the Hijra community made "eunuchs" out of boys,
almost as if to suggest that transness is transferable through physical
proximity.[^9] For instance, in one documented instance, a 12-year-old
boy named Moolah, who was "not emasculated," was found residing with a
person identified as a "eunuch." In the absence of any legal evidence or
substantiation, authorities presumed that he was being subjected to
"unnatural" sexual acts and consequently sent him to a reformatory.

The CT Act itself was repealed as being violative of the spirit of the
Indian constitution. Nonetheless, it appears that recent approaches to
regulating the lives of transgender people through the 2019 and 2026
Acts rely on some of the flawed assumptions and methodologies adopted by
the CT Act.

## Legal Questions

The Transgender (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 was introduced
following the Supreme Court's landmark judgement in NALSA. At a
constitutional level, NALSA formalised a series of rights that focused
on improving the lives of transgender persons. The following are
important from a privacy perspective:

1.  Gender was held to be a core component of one's personal life, and
    therefore, self-identification to be a core component of Article 21
    of the Constitution, breach of which would amount to a breach of the
    right to dignity, liberty and privacy.[^10]

2.  The Court also held that the right to freedom of speech and
    expression under Article 19(1)(a) includes the right to express
    one's self-identified gender through dress, words, actions, or
    behaviour. It stated that the State cannot prohibit or interfere
    with such expressions of personality.[^11]

3.  The judgment explicitly declared that legal gender recognition must
    not be contingent upon Sex Reassignment Surgery (SRS), hormonal
    therapy, or other medical procedures. The Court characterised any
    such insistence by the State as "immoral and illegal."[^12]

In 2018, the Supreme Court in **_K.S Puttuswamy v. Union of
India_**[^13] ("Puttuswamy") upheld the right to privacy as a
fundamental right and recognised the importance of privacy in the realm
of sexual or gender identity, stating:

> "The intersection between one's mental integrity and privacy entitles
> the individual to freedom of thought, the freedom to believe in what
> is right, and the freedom of self-determination. When these guarantees
> intersect with gender, they create a private space which protects all
> those elements which are crucial to gender identity. The family,
> marriage, procreation and sexual orientation are all integral to the
> dignity of the individual. Above all, the privacy of the individual
> recognises an inviolable right to determine how freedom shall be
> exercised."[^14]

Further, the Court also rejected the idea that the rights of the LGBT
Community cannot be provided special consideration since the community
itself holds a miniscule percentage, and often is not accepted by the
majority. It held:

> "The test of popular acceptance does not furnish a valid basis to
> disregard rights which are conferred with the sanctity of
> constitutional protection. Discrete and insular minorities face grave
> dangers of discrimination for the simple reason that their views,
> beliefs or way of life does not accord with the 'mainstream'. Yet in a
> democratic Constitution founded on the rule of law, their rights are
> as sacred as those conferred on other citizens to protect their
> freedoms and liberties."[^15]

Arising from the Puttuswamy judgement was the threefold "Puttuswamy
Test," for laws that place limitations on the Right to Privacy. First,
the state must prove to have a legal mandate and sanction of the law
(legality); second, the restriction must serve a valid state aim, such
as public order or national security (legitimate state aim), and; third,
the restriction must be necessary and proportional to the intended
objective, ensuring it is the least intrusive method possible
(proportionality).

Emerging technologies also lead to increasingly pervasive forms of
surveillance, and, as we noted in the case of the CT Act, surveillance
may act as a form of social control. This was recognised by the Court in
Puttuswamy, where it discussed the "chilling effect" on free speech that
calculated interference with privacy may have. Data collected from
surveillance may also be used to push narratives about minority
communities that impact social perception, and create further cause
reason for surveillance.

Let us analyse, for instance, the Government of India's 2017 Guidelines
that placed an indefinite
[ban](https://naco.mohfw.gov.in/sites/default/files/Letter%20reg.%20%20guidelines%20for%20blood%20donor%20selection%20&%20referral%20-2017.pdf)
on blood transfusion by transgender persons, men who have sex with men,
and female sex workers.[^16] These categories of persons are deemed to
be "at risk" for HIV and Hepatitis B/C, solely on the grounds of their
identity, and are "permanently deferred" them from donating blood.
Naturally, the constitutionality of these Guidelines was challenged
before the Supreme Court of India in _Thangjam Santa Singh \@Santa
Khurai v. Union of India_[^17] on grounds of it being violative pf
Article 14, 15 and 21 of the Constitution.

The Government of India justified said ban, claiming it to be "rooted in
data," and that the priority should be the health of the recipient and
not the "right of the donor," invoking Article 47 of the Constitution
(improvement of public health). Two things we may note here:

First, the "data" cited refers to the 2020-2021 Annual Report of the
Department of Health and Family Welfare which
[stated](https://theprint.in/india/rooted-in-data-how-centre-defended-blood-donation-ban-on-gay-men-sex-workers-trans-people-before-sc/2877646/)
that HIV prevalence in the aforementioned high-risk groups is 6-13 times
higher than average adult HIV prevalence. However, this categorisation
simplifies the interaction of several variables. The United Nations
Development Program Report on Hijras/Transgender Women in India: HIV,
Human Rights and Social Exclusion ("**UNDP Report**")[^18] clarifies
that much of the risk comes from assault and barriers in accessing
treatment services for STIs. This may seem logical—after all,
regardless of the cause for the harm, it is objectively true that the
"high risk people" are of higher risk of these STIs.

However, the issue here is that the ban is not based on what the person
does, but who the person is. The comparison itself is flawed: it pits
the "average adult," who is not routinely exposed to sexual violence or
systemic barriers to healthcare, against the "average trans person,"
whose higher HIV vulnerability often stems from precisely those
structural conditions. Treating this disparity as an inherent feature of
identity collapses cause into category. If the objective is genuinely to
reduce risk, the State should assess specific high-risk
behaviours—such as unprotected sex, transactional sex, or exposure to
sexual violence—rather than imposing blanket bans based on who a
person is. Such identity-based exclusions are overbroad, stigmatising,
and disconnected from the actual drivers of risk.

Second, since activities are now collapsed into identity, categorisation
implies stereotyping. The average transgender person is now perceived as
a likely carrier of diseases such as HIV/AIDS, and are subject to
further stereotyping. This is dangerously reminiscent of the "criminal
tribes" categorisation of the CT Act that discussed before.

Furthermore, this implies mandatory disclosure of sexual history or
gender identity for the purpose of donation of blood. Critics argue that
this is not necessary, since all blood goes through rigorous testing
once donated.[^19] We may best view the Guidelines against the threefold
Puttuswamy Test:

a. Legality: The state does not have the legal mandate to carry out
such a ban since administrative guidelines may not suffice if they
arbitrarily override the fundamental rights established in NALSA and
Puttaswamy.

b. Legitimate State Aim: Puttuswamy recognises public health and the
prevention of disease as legitimate state interests. However, the
test requires that the aim not be manifestly arbitrary.

c. Necessity & Proportionality: Is disclosure of sexual and gender
identity necessary for the purpose of donating blood? Critics argue
that the presence of extensive testing once blood is donating makes
necessary disclosure of SOGI redundant.[^20] Further, it may even
prove to be _counterproductive_ to public health by reducing the
number of blood donors. That is, mandatory disclosure of sensitive
personal information is neither necessary nor proportionate to the
aim of the state in this case, which is to ensure safety of
recipients of donated blood. Non-intrusive alternatives, such as
disclosure of "high-risk behaviours," as opposed to creating a list
of "high-risk persons," do exist and are more likely to pass
constitutional scrutiny.
