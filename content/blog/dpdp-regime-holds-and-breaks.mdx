The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025 ("**the Rules**") have
been notified, consequentially finalising the much-awaited
implementation timeline of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023
("**the Act**"). Stakeholders have reacted differently, with some
hailing this as a watershed moment in India's digital personal data
protection landscape, and others holding more cautionary opinions,
suggesting that while the Rules have indeed addressed some important
concerns that emerged from the Draft Digital Personal Data Protection
Rules 2025 ("**Draft Rules**"), there still are concerns that continue
to exist and require some clarity.
Refer [here ](https://www.notion.so/Digital-Personal-Data-Protection-Rules-2025-2acb990b7e2180e98413e28e0eeefb54?source=copy_link)for
a brief explainer on the Rules themselves.

With respect to the implementation of the Rules itself, the Central
Government has opted for a staggered, three-stage approach to implement
the Act and the Rules over a period of 18 months.

1.  **Stage 1:** Provisions for establishing and governing the Data
    Protection Board of India ("**DPB**" or "**the Board"**), the main
    enforcement body, and rules for appointing its members came into
    effect immediately on November 14, 2025. See **Rules 1, 2 and
    17-21.**

2.  **Stage 2:** Rules for the registration and functioning of Consent
    Managers will be effective after 12 months, allowing time for
    infrastructure development. See **Rule 4.**

3.  **Stage 3:** The majority of compliance requirements for Data
    Fiduciaries, including privacy notices, consent, data security,
    processing children's data, data retention, deletion policies, and
    cross-border data transfers (except to restricted countries), will
    be fully enforceable after 18 months.

This piece analyses in some depth, what works, and what some foreseeable
challenges are, specifically pertaining to compliance, constitutionality
and enforcement. Before we begin, we must be cautious not to let the
best be the enemy of the good. Excessive criticism of what is
undoubtedly a landmark privacy legislation, tailored to Indian
considerations, and expecting prescription of standards will strip away
the room granted for innovation through it.

**What the Framework Does Well**

The first thing the DPDP regime in India ensures is the simplicity of
language to ensure that smaller organisations and the general public are
able to understand and access the framework. The Rules build on this by
standardising notices, creating predictable breach-reporting processes,
and giving users simple digital tools to exercise their rights.

The DPDP framework a standard, clear set of breach-notification duties.
S.8(6), read with Rule 7, requires Data Fiduciaries to inform both the
Data Principal and the Board as soon as practicable, with specified
details. The two-stage model, which involves an initial intimation
followed by a 72-hour report, is broadly consistent with global
practice.

Supplementing this, S.13 and Rule 14 establish a structured and
time-bound grievance process. They require Data Fiduciaries and Consent
Managers to publish contact points and timelines, and to provide clear
digital pathways for exercising rights. For many individuals who
struggle with opaque customer-service systems, this creates a direct and
accessible route for raising privacy concerns, which did not exist under
earlier laws.

Now, although not as wide-ranging as the GDPR, and requiring some
fine-tuning, the Act takes a pragmatic approach to purpose limitation
and retention. The rule that data must be erased once the purpose is
fulfilled, supported by Rule 8 and the Third Schedule, clarifies the
storage limitation framework in India, albeit with some defined
exemptions. The timelines so formed are on the basis of (i) withdrawal
of consent; (ii) expiry of purpose; both of which are clearly defined
under the Act. Abstract standards of "proportionality," and "necessity"
are condensed into clear, actionable requirements.

Although implementation challenges remain, the Act takes a clear
position on the special protection owed to children. Section 9(3)
prohibits tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising
directed at minors. Furthermore, in what has been hailed as an
improvement from the existing regime, the Rules mandate Verifiable
Parental Consent, i.e., consent of an individual who can be verified as
an adult parent in compliance through laws in force, to allow processing
of minors' personal data.

The DPDP framework also fits well with the needs of India's fast-growing
digital economy. The focus on digital governance—online notices,
online grievance systems, online appeals, and a fully digital Data
Protection Board—recognises the country's administrative limits and
avoids creating a regulator that gets overwhelmed. Furthermore, the Act
is designed to operate within India's existing digital public
infrastructure, such as DigiLocker, Aadhaar-based token systems, and
account aggregators. This allows interoperability and more secure data
flows within systems that citizens already use. Few privacy laws
globally have been drafted with a live digital infrastructure in mind,
which makes the DPDP framework distinctive in its approach.

**Challenge 1: Broad State Exemptions**

S.17(2)(a) of the Act gives the Central Government the broad power to
exempt processing carried out by notified state bodies in the interests
of sovereignty, security of the state, friendly relations with foreign
states, public order, or preventing incitement to cognizable offences.
Concerns regarding the broadness of these exemptions and the lack of any
oversight mechanisms
were [raised](https://internetfreedom.in/iffs-first-read-of-the-draft-digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023/?ref=static.internetfreedom.in) upon
the introduction of the DPDP Bill, to no avail. Grounds like "public
order" and "friendly relations with foreign states" are elastic,
enabling expansive interpretation, and the manner of phrasing gives no
room for the checks and balances introduced by the Supreme Court
in [_Justice K.S. Puttuswamy (Retd.) v. Union of
India_](https://www.scobserver.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Right_to_Privacy__Puttaswamy_Judgment_1.pdf) ("**Puttuswamy**"),
which specifically states:

_". ... an invasion of life or personal liberty must meet the three-fold
requirement of (i) legality, which postulates the existence of law; (ii)
need, defined in terms of a legitimate state aim; and (iii)
proportionality which ensures a rational nexus between the objects and
the means adopted to achieve them"_

The fundamental issue is the vagueness of justifications used. Grounds
such as "security of the state," "national sovereignty," etc., enable
the Government to exercise a wide ambit of powers with little to no
oversight. Furthermore, these exemptions may not apply to specific
activities, but to entire classes of processing, and all with the
absence of a clear oversight mechanism. The broadness is not limited to
the range of activities, either. S.17(2)(a) allows entire
instrumentalities to be exempted forever, with no sunset clause, which
would likely enable impunity of the state *ad infinitum*—appearing to
be an extension of the [Government of India's tendency to normalise
emergency
laws.](https://polsci.institute/constitutional-gov-democracy-india/misuse-emergency-provisions-india-history/)

From the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act in the USA, as disclosed
in the Snowden
Files](https://www.theguardian.com/world/interactive/2013/nov/01/snowden-nsa-files-surveillance-revelations-decoded),
which enabled the US Government to collect data on entire populations
(including friendly foreign citizens and heads of state) rather than
being limited to specific, targeted suspects, to the [Social Credit
System of
China](https://jpia.princeton.edu/news/social-credit-system-not-just-another-chinese-idiosyncrasy),
which uses public interest exemptions under the Personal Information
Protection Law for mass data collection, to rate the "trustworthiness"
of citizens and entities, we see [potential for surveillance,
censorship, profiling,
etc](https://internetfreedom.in/iffs-first-read-of-the-draft-digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023/?ref=static.internetfreedom.in).
arising from broad exemptions on state activities vis-à-vis processing
of personal data.

The problem is further exacerbated by Rule 23, which provides the
Central Government with the right to seek information from "any data
fiduciary or intermediary," for certain purposes as specified under
Schedule VII. Further, in a situation where the government deems that
publication or disclosure of such information (read: personal data) is a
threat to public order, the fiduciary shall be barred from informing the
principal about such disclosure. Essentially, not only does the Rule
enable the Government to access essentially any personal data at will,
it also ensures that data principals are unaware of such access, thereby
preventing them from accessing any legal recourse.

That is, S.17(2)(a) creates an exemption without statutory safeguards,
without procedural constraints, and without independent oversight. It
appears to be a textbook violation of both necessity and proportionality
under Puttuswamy because the provision enables bulk exemptions that are
not limited, foreseeable, or reviewable.

The [broadness of the categories of the data that may be accessed invite
abuse](https://internetfreedom.in/iffs-initial-statement-on-the-notification-of-the-digital-data-protection-rules-2025/),
and, in combination with the absence of any meaningful checks and
balances, is antithetical to the language of the Court in Puttuswamy.

Another relevant issue that emerges is Rule 8(3), which requires data
fiduciaries to store data for a period of one-year post-processing, even
if the data principal deletes their account (subject to laws in force).
This "data" includes personal data, associated traffic data and logs of
processing. For significant data fiduciaries, When read with the Third
Schedule, Rule 8(1) mandates maintenance of SDF processing logs for
three years. This is violative of the principles of data minimisation
and storage limitation—internationally accepted best practices which
require that only the minimum necessary amount of data must be
collected, and only for the time period required to carry out the
processing that the data principal has consented to.

This also weakens the data principal's right to erasure, because even if
a user deletes their account, the fiduciary is legally required to keep
their data, and further increases compliance costs and operational
burdens, especially for smaller entities, since long-term secure storage
of logs and traffic data is technically and financially demanding.
Finally, the definition of "traffic data," has not been adequately
addressed by the Rules, creating compliance challenges with storage, and
potentially broadening the scope of the data being stored under this
Rule.

**Challenge 2: Independence of the Data Protection Board of India**

International standards, from
the [EU-GDPR](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-52-gdpr/) to the [World
Bank](https://id4d.worldbank.org/guide/data-protection-and-privacy-laws) recognise
the status of the state as a major data processor, and establish the
necessity of an independent oversight authority to effectively protect
data principal rights. The concept of [genuine
independence](https://www.eiopa.europa.eu/publications/criteria-independence-supervisory-authorities_en) is
aimed at avoiding centralised power, and enabling objective decision
making, and is determined on the basis of a variety of structural
factors, such as method of appointment and allocation of resources. Put
simply, independence ensures that the regulator can:

1.  Make decisions objectively, based solely on data protection law,
    free from political or commercial pressure.

2.  Conduct inquiries and impose sanctions on public authorities, which
    are some of the largest data fiduciaries.

However, the appointment procedure of Data Protection Board of India
("**the Board**") as under Rule 17 states that it shall consist of a
chairperson and members, each of whom shall be appointed by a distinct
search-cum-selection committee ("**committee**"), which recommend
individuals for the corresponding posts. Both selection committees shall
be headed by members of the executive, and the Central Government will
review the recommendations, and, if satisfied, appoint the recommended
individuals in the Board.

Rule 21 empowers the Board to employ officers and employees for
efficient functioning subject to prior permission from the Central Govt,
the Sixth Schedule forms the basis of the Terms and Conditions of
Service of both employees and members of the Board.

Furthermore, S.21 of the Act empowers the Central Government to remove
Chairperson/Members of the Board (subject to a hearing), and requires
resignations of Chairperson/Members to be approved by the Central
Government.

It is evident that the Board lacks the independence outlined under
international norms, given the prevalence of executive discretion in its
functioning given that the Board:

1.  Is established by the Central Government,

2.  Members are appointed by Government,

3.  Service conditions set by Government,

4.  Is removable by Government,

5.  Is dependent on Government for staffing and finances.

Furthermore, the Board also lacks the *suo motu* powers to initiate
inquiry. S.27 of the Act states that Board may begin an inquiry upon
receiving any of the following:

1.  Breach notification from a Data Fiduciary (S.8(6)) - which is also
    communicated to the Principal

2.  Complaint by a Data Principal (breach of Fiduciary obligations or
    Principal rights)

3.  Complaint against a Consent Manager

4.  Intimation of breach of CM registration conditions

S.28(3) provides that the power of the Board with respect to the
initiation of an inquiry is limited to determining whether the
aforementioned triggers contain a *prima facie* case or not. Proactive
enforcement by Data Protection Authorities ("DPAs") have been
instrumental in enforcement of the GDPR across the European Union, such
as in the [Google Analytics
case](https://noyb.eu/en/update-further-eu-dpa-orders-stop-google-analytics),
or [the Interactive Advertising Bureau case in
Belgium](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/national-news/2020/belgian-dpa-imposes-eu20000-fine-proximus-several-data-protection_en).
Further exacerbating the issue is the absence of sufficient
investigative powers. Since the Board cannot conduct raids, seize
equipment, order forensic imaging or enter premises, it relies entirely
on executive cooperation and police support—another hindrance to
genuine independence.

In sum, it may even be argued that the absence of independence, and
executive involvement in the appointment and functioning of the Board is
not merely an enforcement gap; it is a deliberate structural choice that
ensures the Board cannot autonomously scrutinise the State, which is the
single largest data processor in the country.

**Challenge 3: Verifiable Parental Consent**

[For a detailed critique of India's children's data protection
framework, please
see [this](https://articles.manupatra.com/article-details/ENHANCING-CHILDREN-S-DATA-PROTECTION-A-FLEXIBLE-ACCOUNTABLE-AND-RISK-BASED-FRAMEWORK-Author-Siddharth-Aiyar).]

Interestingly, most commentators on the Rules praise the introduction of
the Verifiable Parental Consent model—a perspective I disagree with.
The DPDP Act and Rules adopt a rigid and technologically narrow model of
verifiable parental consent that fails to account for the developmental
diversity of children and the economic and digital realities of Indian
families.

S.9 and Rule 10 establish parental consent as a blanket requirement for
all minors under eighteen, effectively replacing the child's own agency
rather than complementing it. This uniform standard does not recognise
that adolescents possess significantly greater capacity for privacy
decision-making than younger children, nor does it support the gradual
development of data autonomy. As a result, the regime entrenches what
may be termed "data immaturity": children are excluded from privacy
choices throughout their formative years, only to be thrust into full
digital responsibility upon turning eighteen without any
preparation/enabling mechanisms.

The verification mechanisms themselves suffer from serious shortcomings.
Rule 10 restricts verification to two pathways: reuse of identity
information already held by the Data Fiduciary or the use of virtual
tokens issued by authorised entities. Both approaches are deeply
exclusionary in the Indian context, and presume existing digital access
and literacy. Virtual tokens rely on a digital ecosystem that many
parents may not possess: access to DigiLocker, reliable internet, and
sufficient digital literacy. By providing only digital-first
verification options, the Rules may create barriers for low-income and
rural families, threatening to restrict children's participation in
online spaces.

Moreover, the consent framework rests on unrealistic behavioural
assumptions—specifically that children will truthfully disclose their
age, and that parents will have the capacity and willingness to
understand the implications of, and effectively use complex verification
tools. Unlike global models such as COPPA, which permit diverse
verification methods (video calls, postal forms, call-based
confirmations), the DPDP regime provides no flexibility or low-tech
alternatives.

**Conclusion**

Taken together, the DPDP Act and Rules mark a massive leap toward a
functional, nationwide privacy framework, but the picture remains mixed.
Their accessible design, digital-first architecture, and tiered
compliance model show a clear commitment to building a system that can
operate at India's scale. At the same time, the Rules introduce gaps and
tensions that risk diluting core principles like purpose limitation and
placing unnecessary burdens on smaller entities. The core architecture
is sound; the shortcomings lie in oversight design, retention standards,
and a few rigid compliance burdens. These issues are fixable. With
targeted amendments, from narrower state exemptions, and stronger Board
independence, to clearer limits on retention, and more flexible consent
mechanisms, the system can evolve into a balanced, rights-driven regime
that protects users while remaining workable for organisations.
