## India's Approach

As discussed, developing countries—India included—[lack the same
entrenched
institutions](https://af.net/news/the-law-plus-approach-how-legal-frameworks-can-anchor-responsible-ai/#:~:text=For%20developing%20nations%2C%20the,teams%20from%20the%20beginning.)
that enable incorporating AI governance into existing frameworks. There
is general
[agreement](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/11/indias-advance-on-ai-regulation?lang=en#:~:text=Across%20India%E2%80%99s%20government,risk%20use%20cases.)
among stakeholders that, in India, a novel omnibus regulation is likely
to do more harm than good, and that many risks posed by AI can be
addressed through tech-neutral interpretations of existing
laws—barring some novel risks. However, whereas industry stakeholders
have pushed for self-regulation frameworks, academics are
[skeptical](https://www.techpolicy.press/transparency-must-be-a-cornerstone-of-the-digital-india-act/)
about the benefits of the same.

India's approach revolves around three key components: (1)
sector-specific regulations; (2) the India AI Governance Guidelines,
and; (3) the India AI Mission. On (1), appropriate agencies, such as the
[Reserve Bank of
India](https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/PublicationReport/Pdfs/FREEAIR130820250A24FF2D4578453F824C72ED9F5D5851.PDF)
('RBI") for finance, the [Indian Council of Medical
Research](https://www.icmr.gov.in/ethical-guidelines-for-application-of-artificial-intelligence-in-biomedical-research-and-healthcare)
("ICMR") for healthcare, and SEBI for securities
([here](https://www.sebi.gov.in/reports-and-statistics/reports/jun-2025/consultation-paper-on-guidelines-for-responsible-usage-of-ai-ml-in-indian-securities-markets_94687.html)
and
[here](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/meetingfiles/dec-2024/1735042007618_1.pdf)),
take the lead in monitoring harms and enforcing regulations within their
domains.

The [India AI Governance
Guidelines](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc2025115685601.pdf)
take an interesting approach, acting as a philosophical and strategic
roadmap for India. The following components are important to understand:

1.  The guidelines are grounded in seven principles—known as the "7
    sutras"—_Trust_, _People First_, _Innovation over Restraint_,
    _Fairness & Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and
    Safety & Resilience_. They are designed to be technology-agnostic
    and applicable across all sectors. These 7 sutras were drawn from
    the RBI Free-AI Framework, and simply may be understood as:

    a. _Trust is the Foundation_: AI systems must be reliable,
    transparent, and build public confidence. 

    b. _People First_: AI should support humans, prioritize citizen
    welfare, dignity, and inclusion. 

    c. _Innovation over Restraint_: Encourage new ideas while avoiding
    unnecessary restrictions. 

    d. *Fairness and Equity:* AI outcomes must be non-discriminatory
    and equitable. 

    e. _Accountability_: Clearly define responsibility for AI decisions
    and impacts. 

    f. _Understandable by Design_: AI systems and decisions should be
    interpretable for users and regulators. 

    g. _Safety, Resilience, and Sustainability_: AI must be secure,
    adaptable, and sustainable. 

2.  The guidelines propose a "whole-of-government" approach,
    recommending the creation of the AI Governance Group (AIGG) for
    policy coordination and the AI Safety Institute (AISI) for technical
    validation and safety research.

3.  They emphasize "compliance-by-design," using technology
    architectures like Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) to make
    regulatory principles automatically enforceable. This means that
    policy measures are codified into the underlying technical standards
    and protocols of an AI system, including verifiable techno-legal
    safeguards in specific high-risk areas to provide verifiable results

These guidelines supplement the India AI Mission – the third component
of India's AI regulation framework, which [looks
to](https://indiaai.gov.in/) democratize AI benefits and foster
technological self-reliance by operationalising AI development and
adoption. It is built on 7 pillars:

1.  _IndiaAI Innovation Centre_: Focuses on developing and deploying
    indigenous Large Multimodal Models and domain-specific foundational
    models

2.  _IndiaAI Application Development Initiative_: Develops, scales, and
    promotes impactful AI solutions for large-scale socio-economic
    transformation

3.  _AIKosh Platform_: Unified hub center for datasets, models, AI
    sandbox and more to enable AI Innovation

4.  _IndiaAI Compute Capacity_: Focuses on building a scalable AI
    ecosystem with 10,000+ GPUs via public-private partnerships,
    offering AI services and resources

5.  _IndiaAI Startup Financing_: This pillar supports and accelerates
    deep-tech AI startups by providing streamlined access to funding for
    innovative AI projects.

6.  _IndiaAI FutureSkills_: This pillar aims to expand AI education at
    all academic levels and establish Data and AI Labs in Tier 2 and 3
    cities

7.  _Safe & Trusted AI_: Focuses on ensuring responsible AI via
    projects, tools, checklists, and governance frameworks for
    responsible development and use

The Guidelines set out India's thinking on how AI should be governed,
while the Mission provides the infrastructure and institutional support
needed to put that thinking into practice. In simple terms, the
Guidelines define the direction, and the Mission enables execution.

One clear point of overlap is the "Safe and Trusted AI" pillar of the
IndiaAI Mission. The Guidelines describe themselves as providing the
ethical and technical foundation for this pillar. While the Mission
funds practical tools such as systems to detect bias or deepfakes, the
Guidelines lay down the core principles—the seven Sutras—that shape
how these tools should be built and used. This relationship is
operationalised through institutions such as the AI Safety Institute
(AISI), which conducts technical testing and safety research, and works
alongside the AI Governance Group (AIGG) and the Technology & Policy
Expert Committee (TPEC) to translate governance principles into
technical oversight.

The two frameworks also divide responsibilities between enablement and
safeguards. The Mission focuses on building capacity by providing shared
compute resources and datasets, including access to large-scale GPUs and
the AIKosh data platform. The Guidelines, on the other hand, recommend
how this infrastructure should be used to manage risks—such as testing
AI systems for bias at scale and developing standard datasets for safety
evaluation.

Both the Mission and the Guidelines build on India's experience with
Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI). The Mission uses AI to strengthen
public digital systems, including multilingual tools like Bhashini. The
Guidelines ensure that such systems are designed with built-in
safeguards, encouraging privacy, transparency, and accountability to be
embedded directly into the technology rather than added later.

Finally, the two are coordinated through a shared rollout timeline. In
the short term, the Mission expands access to AI infrastructure while
the Guidelines put governance bodies in place. Over time, as AI use
grows across sectors like healthcare and agriculture, the Guidelines
focus on setting standards and reviewing systems to ensure they remain
safe, inclusive, and aligned with public objectives.

Put simply, for now, India does not envision an intensive, enforcement
reliant framework, and instead looks to start building institutions from
a techno-legal perspective, ensuring that decisions are taken on the
basis of regulatory clarity and technical know-how. Here's how India
stands with respect to the 4 primary questions:

1.  **_What objective does the state wish to fulfil through AI
    regulation?_** – The development of the IndiaAI Mission was done so
    with the
    [objective](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2178092&reg=3&lang=2)
    of building a comprehensive ecosystem that drives innovation,
    supports startups, strengthens data access, and ensures the
    responsible use of AI for public good through which India wishes to
    become a "global leader" in AI in 2035.

2.  **_What is AI governed as? --_** India adopts a unique approach,
    clarifying that it does not intend to regulate "AI as a technology"
    but rather AI applications, AI risks, and AI outcomes, primarily
    through sectoral regulators. In other words, AI is treated as an
    application, a risk-vector, an infrastructural medium and an
    economic value chain, depending on sector-specific context. Further,
    India leans more towards a risk-centric approach by organising
    governance primarily around categories of harm—bias,
    misinformation, systemic risk, national security—rather than model
    types or technical thresholds. Put simply—India looks to mainly
    regulate consequences, not the models themselves, which is why AI is
    regulated through many laws but no single statute.

3.  **_Who is responsible for majority of compliance? --_** India's
    approach appears to be a "lifecycle-centric approach," where
    accountability is attributed differently at different parts of the
    AI value chain. Broadly, this approach
    [begins](https://af.net/news/the-law-plus-approach-how-legal-frameworks-can-anchor-responsible-ai/#:~:text=begins%20with%20a%20fundamental%20question%20that%20most%20frameworks%20ignore%3A%20where%20in%20the%20AI%20lifecycle%20are%20you%3F)
    with a fundamental question: where in the AI lifecycle does the
    actor lie? That is, there needs to be clarity on what the role of
    this actor in the AI ecosystem is, and accountability should be
    divided on that basis. Let us look at what this looks like
    [stagewise](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2021-02/Responsible-AI-22022021.pdf):

    a. Stage 1: Conception – This is where the AI system is
    conceptualised. It becomes important to delineate clear ethical
    boundaries and ensuring the intended use case justifies the
    potential risks to users and society.

    b. Stage 2: Pre-training – This is where vast datasets for AI
    training are collected. Accountability looks like auditing
    datasets for historical bias, representative gaps, and consent
    violations.

    c. Stage 3: Training – Here, the datasets are fed into the AI
    system and patterns are internalised. Accountability could
    require rigorous testing for algorithmic fairness and technical
    robustness to ensure the model performs reliably across diverse
    groups.

    d. Stage 4: Deployment – The system is released into the market.
    Deployment must only be done once the developers are satisfied
    that adequate stress testing and audits have been carried out to
    mitigate potential risks. This includes validating what
    guardrails have been set up as well.

    e. Stage 5: Monitoring – This is an accountability mechanism by
    itself – feedback loops are maintained, and in case harm is
    caused/seems likely, there should be a clear channel for human
    intervention.

**Importantly,** this does not mean that the entire load falls on AI
developers. The need for transparency has been recognized across the AI
value chain so regulators can trace harms back to the lifecycle stage
where they arose. This enables stage-specific enforcement, rather than
treating AI harms as indivisible outcomes.

4.  **_Should compliance be ex ante or ex post? --_** This question
    actually loses value, considering that specific compliances are
    envisioned to be sector specific. From a birds-eye view, the MeitY
    has made it
    [clear](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/02/11ab.pdf)
    that India favours a light-touch approach with regulatory sandboxes
    – which primarily means ex-post accountability. Nonetheless, sector
    specific modifications may require ex-ante compliances as well.

Take for instance the [RBI's Free-AI
Framework](https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/PublicationReport/Pdfs/FREEAIR130820250A24FF2D4578453F824C72ED9F5D5851.PDF#page=88.08).
Through Annex IV (AI Specific Enhancements in RBI Master Directions),
the RBI proposes mandatory AI-related pre-emptive compliances for
Registered Entities including AI use disclosure, specific threat
responses and security protocols, transparency requirements, etc. A
proposed Board Policy on AI (Annex V) even recommends incorporating
AI-specific considerations into the administrative functioning of
Registered Entities.

Another example is the proposed
[amendment](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/10/8e40cdd134cd92dd783a37556428c370.pdf)
to the IT Rules 2021, which sets minimum labelling requirements for
synthetically generated information, establishing platform
accountability for identifying AI content, and defining obligations for
intermediaries (like social media) to remove unlawful synthetic content,
aiming for transparency and user safety while increasing compliance for
tech companies.

This, however, brings up some important questions. It has been
[clarified](https://visionias.in/blog/current-affairs/indias-new-ai-governance-guidelines-push-hands-off-approach#:~:text=7%20Sutras%20of%20India's%20AI%20Governance%20Fairness,in%20AI%20systems%20for%20users%20and%20regulators.)
that "transparency" does not mean full model disclosure or source-code
openness, and is only limited to making the roles, decisions, and
safeguards at each stage of the AI lifecycle visible enough for
accountability and enforcement. Further, the previously discussed
_ex-ante_ compliances work excellently for the training, testing and
deployment stage. However, what does this accountability really _look_
like at the pre-training and training stage? That is to say, whereas
there exists clarity on question such as who trained the model, or who
is responsible for emergent harms, there is minimal clarity on to
_preventive_ checks. This is especially important considering that some
harms (such as the Grok fiasco discussed earlier) are a result of human
actions and can trample over the dignity and rights of
individuals—making _ex post_ accountability mechanisms of minimal
value. Matters of criminal liability, for instance, cannot be
sector-specific developments, but balancing the need for guardrails with
innovation has been envisioned to be the question of the hour.

Another question that has yet to be answered is the nature of data used.
India's data economy is vast, and the datasets used include copyrighted
information, personal data, and other data that may be protected in any
other way. The DPIIT has
[attempted](https://www.dpiit.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/12/ff266bbeed10c48e3479c941484f3525.pdf)
to address concerns regarding training on copyrighted data, but this
attempt seems premature and with some
[flaws](https://everythingtmt.substack.com/p/who-pays-when-ai-learns)
that yet need ironing out. Nevertheless, the bigger concern is the usage
of personal data in AI training, often scraped from public sources like
social media. This raises significant privacy
[concerns](https://www.dentonsdata.com/use-of-personal-data-in-training-and-deployment-of-ai/)
regarding consent, repurposing, and potential re-identification,
[requiring](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/4022261/how-to-use-ai-and-personal-data.pdf)
strict data minimisation, anonymisation, pseudonymisation. Scholars
[predict](https://law.asia/ai-and-data-protection/#:~:text=Given%20the%20broad%20definition%20of%20the%20term%20%E2%80%9Cprocessing%E2%80%9D%2C%20the%20DPDP%20Act%20will%20also%20apply%20to%20instances%20where%20personal%20data%20is%20collected%20and%20processed%20for%20the%20development%20and%20training%20of%20AI%20models.)
that the DPDP Regime will be interpreted to govern training and
pre-training given the "broad definition of the term 'processing'."

However, at the end of the day, India is genuinely doing something
right. The Governance Guidelines are unique, flexible and recognize
India's ground realities. Unlike the USA, EU or China, India's approach
focuses on capacity building along with some guardrails, [and views AI
from the lens of Digital Public
Infrastructure](<https://www.techpolicy.press/indias-new-ai-governance-plan-is-much-ado-about-nothing/#:~:text=views%20all%20emerging%20technologies%20from%20the%20lens%20of%20digital%20public%20infrastructure%20(DPI).%20The%20issue%20of%20infrastructure%20lends%20itself%20naturally%20to%20AI%20policymaking%20%E2%80%94%20the%20issues%20of%20developing%20GPUs%2C%20increasing%20access%20to%20datasets%20and%20expanding%20AI%20infrastructure%20feature%20prominently.>).
This is visible in clear recommendations to link AI with Aadhaar, UPI
and DigiLocker. Critics
[insist](https://www.techpolicy.press/indias-new-ai-governance-plan-is-much-ado-about-nothing/)
that the Governance Guidelines are inadequate, since there are no
binding, "real" actions that have been introduced. While well-placed,
these criticisms bring two real risks:

1.  While the "innovation-regulation" dichotomy is
    [flawed](https://www.zeropartydata.es/p/innovation-or-regulation-and-other),
    premature risk-prevention measures pose the risk of contradicting
    the broader nature and direction of AI governance proposed. Where
    governance is still evolving at the level of principles,
    institutions, and technical capacity, early hard-coding of
    obligations may constrain future regulatory flexibility rather than
    enhance protection.

2.  Excessive granularity will lead to incomplete regulation. The DPIIT
    Working Paper, for example, attempts to address the very granular
    harms arising from usage of copyrighted information in datasets.
    This is a highly contested downstream issue, but the steps
    recommended by the DPIIT are not supported by a general framework
    governing datasets as a distinct regulatory object. Questions of
    data provenance, consent, representativeness, licensing, personal
    data protection, and cross-border data flows remain largely
    unaddressed.

## Final Thoughts

India's approach to AI governance is distinct not because it regulates
less, but because it regulates differently. Rather than rushing into an
omnibus AI law, India has chosen to first build institutional capacity,
technical expertise, and shared infrastructure. For a developing country
without deeply entrenched regulatory systems, this sequencing is
pragmatic and well-judged.

The India AI Governance Guidelines set the principles and direction,
while the IndiaAI Mission provides the tools and institutions to put
those principles into practice. Together, they reflect a
lifecycle-based, sector-led, and risk-centric model that keeps
regulatory options open at a time when AI technology and its harms are
still evolving. The focus on Digital Public Infrastructure and
compliance-by-design allows governance to scale through architecture,
not just enforcement.

The open questions around training-stage accountability, data use, and
dignity-based harms are not failures of this approach. They are the
pressure points of a framework that has deliberately postponed rigid
rules in favour of flexibility and learning. Whether India can address
these issues without losing that flexibility will determine how this
model matures.

India also has a rare advantage: the freedom to start fresh. Without
legacy regulatory baggage, it can design AI governance around today's
realities rather than yesterday's laws. If calibrated carefully, India's
approach offers a credible alternative to both heavy-handed regulation
and unchecked market-led development.
