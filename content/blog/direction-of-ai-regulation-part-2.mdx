## Regulatory Perspectives

A second deceptively simple question arises here: ***what exactly is AI
regulation?*** In a related vein, in the late 1990s, governance of the
cyberspace was subject to a debate in the USA—popularised as the ["law
of the horse" debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Horse).
The premise was fairly simple: Judge Easterbrook
[argued](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?referer=&httpsredir=1&article=2147&context=journal_articles)
that despite all the harms that horses (an allegory for the cyberspace)
posed to humans—there was no separate law for "horses," and that all
these harms were addressed through traditional legal frameworks, such as
torts. This was countered by Lawrence Lessig, who
[asserted](https://cyber.harvard.edu/works/lessig/LNC_Q_D2.PDF) that
distinct harms emerge from the cyberspace since the environment of
cyberspace that differs from an analogous feature in real space. In the
context of AI, similar questions arise: should separate AI regulations
exist? Or should existing laws be adapted to address novel harms brought
up by AI?

Regulatory directions have been wide-ranging, with countries taking
different decisions as a result of differing economic and political
priorities. From the USA's [innovation-first
approach](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/#:~:text=Section%201.,%2C%20particularly%20for%20start%2Dups.),
to the EU's [risk-proportionate
approach](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
and China's [big-state
approach](https://carnegieendowment.org/emissary/2025/09/ai-china-90-percent-economy-why-wont-work?lang=en),
AI regulation in the technologically developed world has already begun
to take a concrete shape, often using incumbent institutional
frameworks—a luxury not afforded to developing nations.

### The United States of America – Innovation First, America First

President Donald Trump has been extremely
[vocal](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/08/us/politics/trump-artificial-intelligence.html)
about the necessity of removing barriers to AI innovation, and this has
been the general direction taken by the United States under his
leadership. Broadly, while the USA lacks a single legislation/statute
like the European Union, its approach to AI regulation relies on [two
primary
strategies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-risk-regulation/article/towards-intelligent-regulation-of-artificial-intelligence/AF1AD1940B70DB88D2B24202EE933F1B):
establishing guidelines and standards through federal agencies and
encouraging self-regulation within the industry.

The uniquely federal structure of the USA also enables states to make
laws governing AI—which will become far too much for us to analyse. To
best understand the American approach, we will examine two Federal
frameworks: [America's AI Action Plan
(2025)](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf#page=6.09)
("Action Plan"), and [Executive Order
14365](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/)
(Ensuring A National Policy Framework For Artificial Intelligence)
(2025) ("Order").

The Action Plan, a non-binding document released by the US Government,
revolves around 3 pillars: AI Innovation, AI Infrastructure, and
International AI Diplomacy. At its core, the Action Plan treats AI
regulation as an exercise in enabling experimentation rather than
constraining deployment. It emphasises reducing regulatory friction,
promoting open-source and open-weight AI development, expanding
regulatory sandboxes, and accelerating AI adoption in priority sectors.

The remaining pillars—AI Infrastructure and International AI
Diplomacy—extend this logic beyond regulation. The former sees AI as
an industrial policy problem, looking to develop compute capacity,
energy availability, and semiconductor security. The latter treats AI
governance as a geopolitical contest, seeking to export American
technical standards while countering rival regulatory and technological
influence—which we generally infer to mean China and Russia. This
adversarial approach to geopolitics is somewhat of a hallmark of
President Trump's politics, and likely represents an isolationist
approach to standards setting and quality control for AI.

The Order takes a somewhat aggressive step in this direction, looking to
harmonise state laws on AI. The premise of this Order is fairly direct:
fragmented laws on AI impact innovation, since they make compliance for
interstate functionality of AI entities, especially when such laws are
excessively onerous. The Government looks to enforce this by making
funding under the Broadband Equity Access and Deployment (BEAD) Program
[for internet and broadband development] subject to compliance.

Critics
[observe](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/trumps-ai-order-faces-political-legal-hurdles-2025-12-12/)
that the Order goes beyond "excessively onerous" laws, and looks to
prohibit any law that it feels is not a "minimally burdensome policy
framework"—an obviously over-broad term. Nonetheless, this Order is an
excellent representation of the USA's approach – innovation and US
dominance, sometimes even [at the cost of protections and
safety](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/08/us/politics/trump-artificial-intelligence.html).

In fact, through [Executive Order
14179](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/removing-barriers-to-american-leadership-in-artificial-intelligence/)
(Removing Barriers To American Leadership In Artificial Intelligence),
revoked Executive Order 14110 (Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development
and Use of Artificial Intelligence), which was introduced by the Biden
administration and looked to create a rights-first-innovation-next
approach to development of AI. The US government asserted that Order
14110 was "foreshadowed an onerous regulatory regime," and revocation
was necessary for innovation and US dominance in the "[AI
war](https://www.financialexpress.com/world-news/us-news/whoever-wins-ai-war-will-lead-the-world-trump-predicts-so-many-jobs-we-wont-have-enough-people/4101722/)."

### European Union – Rights First (?)

The European Union has taken the most ambitious regulatory step globally
through the [EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu)—the
world's first comprehensive, binding framework for artificial
intelligence. Unlike the United States' enablement-first strategy, the
AI Act adopts a [precautionary, risk-based approach]{.underline},
structuring compliances and obligation around the potential harm posed
by different AI systems, with the explicit objective of safeguarding
fundamental rights while permitting controlled innovation. At the time
of implementation in 2024, it was seen as an
[extension](https://www.techpolicy.press/eu-set-the-global-standard-on-privacy-and-ai-now-its-pulling-back/#:~:text=The%20plan%20is%20presented%20as,AI%20regulation%20are%20approached%20worldwide.)
of the EU's attempt to set global standards for rights-centric
technology, just as it did with the GDPR.

At the heart of the AI Act lies a [four-tier risk
classification](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/) system,
ranging from minimal risk to unacceptable risk. Systems deemed to pose
"unacceptable" risks—such as social scoring, manipulative or deceptive
techniques that impair informed decision-making, and certain forms of
biometric categorisation—are prohibited outright. High-risk AI
systems, by contrast, are permitted only subject to stringent ex ante
obligations.

Crucially, the AI Act places the primary burden of compliance on AI
developers, particularly in relation to high-risk systems, while also
imposing defined obligations on professional users who deploy such
systems. Its jurisdictional reach is deliberately expansive, applying to
any AI system placed on or affecting the EU market, regardless of where
the developer or deployer is located—a similar approach to that of the
GDPR.

Another seminal provision is [Article
50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/), which mandates
transparency in AI systems—specifically that AI systems are developed
and used in a way that allows appropriate traceability and
explainability. This means two things: (1) the broad logic followed by
the model (how it came up with a decision) is to be fairly
understandable, and; (2) a response/decision should be traceable to an
AI system if generated by one. This may include labelling, metadata or
any other technically feasible and interoperable measure.

However, the
[introduction](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/digital-omnibus-regulation-proposal)
of the proposed EU Digital Omnibus Directive ("Omnibus Directive") and
the AI Omnibus Directive ("AI Omnibus") in November 2025 as a way
to [simplify compliance and reduce
bureaucracy](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-collects-feedback-simplify-rules-data-cybersecurity-and-artificial-intelligence-upcoming)
raises concerns regarding the sanctity of the EU's rights-first approach
(hence the "?" in the subheading.) The Omnibus Directive looks to amend
large parts of the European Union's existing digital
rulebook—including the GDPR and ePrivacy Directive and key
cybersecurity laws.

Amendments under the Omnibus Directive have been
[greeted](https://noyb.eu/en/digital-omnibus-first-legal-analysis) with
concern and scepticism—specifically the proposed Article 9(2)(k) of
the GDPR and Article 88c of the Omnibus Directive. Essentially, while
the GDPR generally envisions a citizen-first approach, this modification
will likely enable mass collection and processing of personal data for
*any* component of AI development without needing explicit permission of
the owner of the data, subject to some conditions. In fact, analyses
suggest that the European Commission makes two legally invalid
assumptions: (1) that the presence of a commercial interest (in
developing AI) implies the presence of a legitimate interest (this has
been categorically denied by the CJEU); (2) that a legitimate interest
always exists in the case of development and operation of AI systems.

The AI Omnibus specifically amends part of the EU AI Act, but the
amendments proposed are limited to enforcement timelines and regulatory
relaxations. Three changes are important to note: (1) high-risk
obligations deferred until clear standards are set; (2) shift from a
binding obligation on AI developers to a soft duty on Member States and
the Commission to "encourage" AI literacy; (3) broad legal basis for
processing sensitive personal data for bias detection in all AI systems.
Fortunately, the AI Omnibus does not abandon the EU's rights-first
philosophy—but it softens its operational absolutism.

### China – The State Knows Best

The Chinese approach has been characterised by quick regulatory
responses to new technologies, with much regulatory power in the hands
of the state,
[including](https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2023/07/03/china-us-ai-technology-chatgpt/)
an extensive range of obligations imposed on tech firms-ranging from
content moderation to data protection, intellectual property (IP), and
ethical issues. Industry experts suggest that the cautious, heavy-handed
approach undertaken by China is likely an anathema to open innovation,
even [going so far as to
say](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/china-tech/china-tells-big-tech-companies-not-to-offer-chatgpt-services)
that it the Chinese state itself is limiting the extent to which
innovation of Generative AI models for fear of losing power. Others
[observe](https://www.jtl.columbia.edu/volume-63/the-promise-and-perils-of-chinas-regulation-of-artificial-intelligence)
that authoritarian states see tech regulation as a fine line: while on
one hand technological advances can empower civil society by reducing
the cost of collective mobilization against authoritarian rule, on the
other, such advances can improve the state's surveillance and enable
pre-emptive crackdowns on anti-state mobilisation, and also enable
innovation and development—improving the regime's legitimacy.

Broadly, it helps to understand China's approach to digital regulation
(including AI) as focusing on granular control of information flows,
traceability of AI outputs, and iterative administrative regulation
rather than a single omnibus AI law. Law is less rights-centric or
innovation-centric and more focused on maintaining social order. This
direction becomes clear when the [Interim Measures for the Management of
Generative Artificial Intelligence Services
(2023)](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2023-07/13/c_1690898327029107.htm), the
[Administration of Deep Synthesis of Internet Information Services
Regulations
(2023)](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2022-12/11/c_1672221949354811.htm), the
[Regulations on the Administration of Algorithm Recommendations for
Internet Information Services
(2022)](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2022-01/04/c_1642894606364259.htm), and
the [Measures for Labeling Artificial Intelligence-Generated
Content](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2025-03/14/c_1743654684782215.htm) are
read together.

Across all four instruments, China does not regulate AI as an
independent technological system. Instead, it regulates:

1.  information outputs,

2.  distribution channels, and

3.  their effects on public opinion and social order.

The Interim Measures, Deep Synthesis Regulations, Algorithm
Recommendation Rules, and the Labelling Rules all converge on the same
regulatory object: AI-mediated information circulation.

The labelling regime makes this explicit. By mandating both explicit
labels (visible/audible) and implicit labels (embedded metadata with
provider identity and content ID), China is constructing a traceable
information environment, not just a transparency regime in the
rights-based sense. The obligation imposed on distribution platforms to
detect, classify, and reinforce labels shows that governance
responsibility follows content downstream, not just upstream at the
model developer level.

Further, as opposed to the EU AI Act's transparency requirements, it is
clear that China prefers traceability over explainability. The four
instruments mandate:

1.  explicit and implicit labels, including embedded metadata
    identifying the service provider and content ID, and require
    platforms to detect, classify, and reinforce those labels even where
    AI generation is only possible or suspected;

2.  marking AI-generated content, ensure traceability, retrain models
    after violations, and submit to security assessments;

3.  technical identifiers (such as watermarks), prohibiting their
    removal, and requiring real-name registration and content review;

4.  disclosures when algorithms are being used and an option for users
    to opt out.

However, unlike the EU, there is minimal focus on making systems
transparent or explainable to the user.

Further, China has adopted what we may understand as a "custodial
responsibility" model—which requires constant compliance by AI
developers (also referred to as "providers"). This is a reflection of
the "ex-ante" approach
[associated](https://www.jtl.columbia.edu/volume-63/the-promise-and-perils-of-chinas-regulation-of-artificial-intelligence)
with China. This means that:

1.  Providers are deemed content producers (Interim Measures).

2.  They must prevent harms *ex ante*, retrain models after violations,
    and block recurrence.

3.  Platforms must maintain harmful content libraries, conduct input and
    output review, and intervene even without user complaints (Deep
    Synthesis Regulations).

4.  Algorithms shaping public opinion require registration and security
    assessment before deployment.

An interesting aspect to note here is that unlike other regulators, the
Chinese government is [entrenched in the AI ecosystem
itself](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-forum-on-ai-law-and-governance/article/navigating-chinas-regulatory-approach-to-generative-artificial-intelligence-and-large-language-models/969B2055997BF42DE693B7A1A1B4E8BA).
In addition to acting as a regulator, the government acts as a
policymaker, an investor, a supplier and an end-user of AI systems. A
very interesting analysis of what some scholars refer to as the
"[Chinese Triple Helix
framework](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308596120300525)"
explains the nature of this model—where conceptualisation in
universities is replaced with direct government involvement in
production-stage technologies.

The three models analyses broadly explain the approach of the
technologically developed world. Broadly, these models differ on the
basis of the answers of the following questions:

1.  What is the objective the state wishes to fulfil through AI
    governance?

2.  What is AI governed as?

3.  Who is responsible for majority of compliance?

4.  Should compliance be ex ante or ex post?
