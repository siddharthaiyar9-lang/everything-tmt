## Overview and Legislative Context

The **Information Technology Act, 2000 ("IT Act")** is India's primary legislation governing electronic records, digital transactions, cybersecurity, intermediary liability, and several forms of cybercrime. It was enacted to provide a legal framework for an increasingly digital economy, where communication, commerce, and government services were rapidly moving online.

Before the IT Act came into force, Indian law was largely designed for a paper-based world. While electronic communication was becoming more common, there was no comprehensive legal framework recognising electronic records or digital signatures. This created uncertainty around the validity of online transactions, electronic contracts, and digital evidence.

The IT Act addressed this gap by giving legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures, allowing electronic transactions to enjoy the same legal validity as their paper-based counterparts. It also established a regulatory framework for certifying digital signatures, created civil and criminal liability for various cyber offences, and introduced mechanisms for adjudication and enforcement in matters involving information technology.

The Act was based largely on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce, 1996**, which sought to harmonise electronic commerce laws across different jurisdictions. India adopted many of its core principles, particularly the idea that electronic records and electronic communications should not be denied legal effect merely because they exist in digital form.

The IT Act came into force on **17 October 2000**. However, it has evolved considerably since then.

Over time, technological developments exposed several gaps in the original legislation. The internet had expanded beyond electronic commerce into social media, online platforms, cloud computing, digital payments, and large-scale data processing. As cyber threats became more sophisticated, the original Act was no longer sufficient to address the realities of India's digital ecosystem.

To respond to these developments, Parliament enacted the **Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008**, which significantly expanded the scope of the legislation. Among other things, the amendment:

- strengthened the framework governing cyber offences;
- introduced data protection obligations for body corporates under Section 43A;
- established the legal basis for **CERT-In** as India's national cyber incident response agency;
- expanded the Government's powers relating to interception, monitoring, decryption, and blocking of online content;
- modernised provisions relating to digital signatures and electronic authentication; and
- introduced the modern framework governing intermediary liability and the safe harbour protection available under Section 79.

Today, the IT Act regulates far more than electronic commerce. It forms the backbone of India's digital legal framework and interacts with numerous subordinate rules, including:

- the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021;
- the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011 (SPDI Rules);
- the Blocking Rules, 2009;
- the Monitoring Rules, 2009;
- the Traffic Data Rules, 2009; and
- several rules governing digital signatures and Certifying Authorities.

The IT Act has also influenced amendments to several other statutes. To ensure that India's legal system could recognise and rely upon electronic records, it amended multiple existing laws, including:

- the Indian Penal Code, 1860;
- the Indian Evidence Act, 1872;
- the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891; and
- the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.

These amendments ensured that electronic records, digital evidence, and electronic transactions could operate within India's broader legal system.

## Why the IT Act is Criticised Today

Although the IT Act was a landmark piece of legislation when enacted, technology has evolved far more quickly than the law itself.

Many provisions were drafted before the emergence of smartphones, artificial intelligence, social media platforms, cloud computing, blockchain technology, and modern digital ecosystems. As a result, several provisions now struggle to address contemporary technological challenges.

Critics have also argued that some provisions grant broad powers to the Government, particularly in relation to surveillance, interception, monitoring, and blocking of online content. Others point to gaps in the Act's treatment of emerging technologies and the absence of a comprehensive, modern framework for digital governance.

## The Future: Towards the Digital India Act

Recognising these limitations, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) has proposed replacing the IT Act with a new **Digital India Act**.

The proposed legislation is expected to adopt a more technology-neutral and future-oriented approach, addressing issues such as online safety, emerging technologies, artificial intelligence, platform regulation, and modern digital governance.

However, the Digital India Act has not yet been introduced in Parliament. Until it is enacted and brought into force, the **Information Technology Act, 2000**—together with the rules framed under it—continues to serve as the principal legislation governing information technology in India.

It is also important to read the IT Act alongside newer statutes, particularly the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)**, which establishes India's dedicated framework for personal data protection. Together, these statutes form the foundation of India's evolving digital regulatory landscape.

## Commonly Used Terms in the Information Technology Act, 2000

Before diving into the substantive provisions of the IT Act, it is helpful to understand some of the key terms used throughout the legislation. Many of these definitions appear repeatedly in later chapters, so having a clear understanding of them will make the rest of the Act much easier to follow.

<Definition title="1. Computer (Section 2(1)(i))">

### What does it mean?

The IT Act defines a **computer** very broadly. It is **any electronic, magnetic, optical, or other high-speed data processing device** that can receive, process, store, retrieve, or transmit data.

Importantly, the definition is not limited to a desktop or laptop computer. It also includes every component that allows the device to function, such as:

- input devices (keyboard, mouse, scanner);
- output devices (monitor, printer);
- storage devices (hard drives, SSDs, pen drives);
- software and operating systems; and
- communication facilities such as network connections or internet-enabled components.

The legislature deliberately adopted a wide definition so that the law would continue to apply as technology evolved.

### Why does this matter?

Almost every offence under the IT Act begins with the use of a **computer**, **computer system**, or **computer resource**. Because these terms are interpreted broadly, the Act applies to far more devices than traditional computers.

<CaseLaw title="Judicial interpretation">

In **Syed Asifuddin & Ors. v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2006)**, the Court held that **mobile phones are also "computers"** for the purposes of the IT Act.

The Court reasoned that a mobile phone performs the same essential functions as a computer—it processes data using programmed computer chips, converts signals into digital information, stores data, and executes software. As technology has evolved, this interpretation has become even more significant, since modern smartphones are essentially portable computers.

</CaseLaw>

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Computer System (Section 2(1)(j))">

### What does it mean?

A **computer system** refers to one or more interconnected computers and related devices that work together to process data according to a set of programmed instructions.

A computer system typically includes:

- one or more computers;
- input devices;
- output devices;
- storage devices;
- software; and
- communication equipment.

The definition excludes simple calculators or devices that cannot be programmed or cannot process externally supplied data.

### Why does this matter?

Many offences under the IT Act relate not merely to a single computer but to an entire system. For example, hacking into a company's internal server or compromising an organisation's network would generally involve a computer system rather than just one individual computer.

</Definition>

<Definition title="3. Asymmetric Cryptosystem (Section 2(1)(f))">

### What does it mean?

An **asymmetric cryptosystem** is a method of encryption that uses **two different cryptographic keys**:

- a **public key**, which anyone can access; and
- a **private key**, which is known only to its owner.

The two keys work together.

Information encrypted using one key can only be decrypted using the other corresponding key.

Unlike traditional encryption systems that use a single key for both encryption and decryption, asymmetric cryptography separates these two functions, making digital communication significantly more secure.

<Example title="Example">

Suppose **A** wants to send a confidential message to **B**.

- A encrypts the message using **B's public key**.

- Even if someone intercepts the message, they will only see encrypted data (called **ciphertext**).

- Only **B's private key** can decrypt the message.

- As a result, only B can read the original message.

This is the technology that makes secure online communication possible.

</Example>

### How does this relate to Digital Signatures?

The same technology also forms the foundation of **digital signatures**.

A digital signature is designed to prove three things:

1. the document genuinely came from the claimed sender;
2. the sender's identity can be verified; and
3. the document has not been altered after it was signed.

### How does a digital signature work?

Imagine A wants to digitally sign a document.

### Step 1: The document is processed using a hash function

A hash function converts the entire document into a fixed-length value called a **hash**. Depending on the algorithm used, this may be 128 bits, 256 bits, or another fixed length.

A hash is often described as the document's **digital fingerprint**.

Even changing a single character in the document will produce a completely different hash.

### Step 2: A encrypts this hash using A's private key

This encrypted hash becomes the digital signature.

**Step 3:** The signed document is sent to the recipient.

**Step 4:** The recipient independently generates a new hash of the received document.

### Step 5: The recipient decrypts A's digital signature using A's public key

If both hash values are identical, the recipient knows that:

- the document has not been altered;
- the signature genuinely belongs to A; and
- the sender cannot later deny signing the document (known as **non-repudiation**).

This technology forms the backbone of electronic contracts, digital authentication, and electronic evidence under the IT Act.

</Definition>

<Definition title="4. Digital Signature (Sections 2(1)(p), 3 and 3A)">

A **digital signature** is an electronic method of verifying the authenticity of an electronic record.

Instead of signing a paper document with ink, a person signs an electronic document using cryptographic technology based on asymmetric encryption.

Section 5 of the IT Act grants legal recognition to digital signatures.

However, digital signatures cannot be issued by just anyone.

Only entities licensed by the **Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA)** under **Section 21** may issue **Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs)**.

Before granting a licence, the CCA examines whether the applicant satisfies the prescribed technical, financial, and operational requirements, including adequate infrastructure, security standards, and qualified personnel.

<CaseLaw title="Judicial recognition">

In **Trimex International FZE Ltd. v. Vedanta Aluminium Ltd. (2010)**, the Supreme Court held that a contract concluded entirely through email correspondence was legally enforceable.

The Court observed that the absence of a signed physical contract did not invalidate the agreement. Since both parties had agreed on all essential contractual terms through authenticated electronic communications, a valid contract existed.

The decision reaffirmed that electronic communications and digital signatures can satisfy legal requirements traditionally fulfilled by paper documents.

</CaseLaw>

</Definition>

<Definition title="5. Electronic Record (Section 2(1)(t))">

An **electronic record** includes any information that is created, stored, received, or transmitted electronically.

This includes:

- emails;
- text messages;
- digital photographs;
- videos;
- audio recordings;
- electronic documents;
- databases;
- computer files;
- information stored on servers;
- microfilm; and
- computer-generated microfiche.

### Why is this important?

The IT Act gives electronic records legal recognition that is broadly equivalent to traditional paper documents.

Without this recognition, online contracts, digital evidence, electronic banking, and e-governance would not function effectively.

<CaseLaw title="Judicial recognition">

In **Shafhi Mohammad v. State of Himachal Pradesh (2018)**, the Supreme Court highlighted the importance of adapting the law to modern technology, including emerging forms of electronic evidence such as body-worn cameras.

Although the case primarily concerned evidentiary procedure, it reflects the broader objective of the IT Act: ensuring that electronic records receive legal recognition while maintaining safeguards against manipulation and misuse.

</CaseLaw>

</Definition>

<Definition title="6. Intermediary (Section 2(1)(w))">

An **intermediary** is any person or entity that receives, stores, transmits, or provides services relating to electronic records on behalf of another person.

Common examples include:

- internet service providers (ISPs);
- social media platforms;
- messaging services;
- web-hosting providers;
- cloud service providers; and
- online marketplaces.

Intermediaries generally do not create the content they host or transmit. Instead, they provide the infrastructure through which users communicate and share information.

Because of this unique role, the IT Act grants intermediaries certain legal protections while also imposing specific obligations. These protections are primarily found in **Section 79** and the **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021**, both of which are discussed in detail later in this guide.

</Definition>
## Legal Recognition of Electronic Records and Digital Signatures

One of the primary objectives of the Information Technology Act, 2000 was to remove the legal uncertainty surrounding electronic transactions. Before the Act was enacted, Indian law largely assumed that important documents would exist in physical form and would bear handwritten signatures. As business and government increasingly shifted online, this approach became impractical.

The IT Act addresses this problem by recognising electronic records and digital signatures as legally valid. In simple terms, the Act ensures that a document does not lose its legal value merely because it exists in digital form instead of on paper.

## Section 4 – Legal Recognition of Electronic Records

### What does Section 4 provide?

Section 4 states that where any law requires information to be:

- in writing;
- typewritten; or
- printed,

that requirement is satisfied if the information is made available in an **electronic record**, provided that the record is accessible and can be referred to whenever required.

In other words, a document cannot be rejected simply because it exists in electronic form.

### Why is this important?

Section 4 is the legal foundation for India's digital economy.

Without it:

- emails would not have the same legal standing as letters;
- electronic contracts could be challenged merely because they were not printed;
- online banking records would have limited legal value;
- e-governance would become extremely difficult; and
- businesses would still need to maintain paper copies of almost every important document.

By giving legal recognition to electronic records, the Act allows individuals, businesses, and government departments to conduct transactions electronically with confidence.

<Example title="Practical example">

Suppose two companies negotiate a commercial agreement entirely through email. They agree on all essential terms, exchange signed PDF documents, and begin performing their obligations.

The agreement does not become invalid simply because no physical document was signed. Section 4 ensures that the electronic records themselves are capable of satisfying legal requirements relating to written documents.

</Example>

## Section 5 – Legal Recognition of Digital Signatures

While Section 4 recognises electronic documents, Section 5 recognises **digital signatures**.

Where any law requires a person's signature, that requirement is satisfied if the document is authenticated using a digital signature or any other electronic authentication technique recognised under the Act.

This places properly authenticated electronic signatures on the same legal footing as handwritten signatures.

### Why are digital signatures important?

A handwritten signature mainly proves that a person intended to sign a document.

A digital signature goes much further.

It helps establish:

- who signed the document;
- whether the document has been altered after signing;
- whether the signature is genuine; and
- whether the signer can later deny having signed it (known as **non-repudiation**).

These safeguards make digital signatures significantly more secure than ordinary handwritten signatures in many situations.

### Digital Signatures vs Electronic Signatures

Many people use these terms interchangeably, but they are **not the same**.

A **digital signature** is a specific type of electronic signature that uses cryptographic technology, particularly asymmetric encryption and hash functions.

An **electronic signature** is a broader concept. It includes any electronic method of indicating approval or consent that satisfies the legal requirements under Section 3A of the IT Act.

Every digital signature is an electronic signature.

However, not every electronic signature is a digital signature.

### Sections 3 and 3A – Methods of Authentication

## Section 3 – Digital Signatures

Section 3 recognises authentication through digital signatures based on asymmetric cryptography and hash functions. This remains the traditional and most secure method of authentication under the IT Act.

## Section 3A – Electronic Signatures

Technology continues to evolve, and Parliament recognised that future authentication methods might not rely solely on traditional digital signatures.

Accordingly, Section 3A allows the Central Government to notify additional forms of electronic authentication, provided they satisfy prescribed standards of reliability, security, and integrity.

This ensures that the law remains technologically neutral and can adapt to future developments without requiring fresh legislation every time authentication technology evolves.

## Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs)

A digital signature can only be trusted if the recipient knows that it genuinely belongs to the claimed individual.

This is where a **Digital Signature Certificate (DSC)** becomes important.

A DSC functions much like a digital identity card. It links a person's identity with their public cryptographic key and allows others to verify that a digital signature genuinely belongs to that individual.

Digital Signature Certificates are issued only by **Certifying Authorities** licensed under the IT Act.

These authorities operate under the supervision of the **Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA)**, who regulates their functioning and ensures compliance with the standards prescribed under the Act.

### Why These Provisions Matter

Sections 3, 3A, 4, and 5 collectively form the legal backbone of electronic commerce in India, without which these provisions, countless everyday activities would face legal uncertainty, including:

- online banking;
- electronic contracts;
- e-commerce transactions;
- digital filing before government authorities;
- corporate compliance filings;
- GST and income tax returns;
- electronic procurement; and
- digital governance initiatives.

These provisions ensure that the law focuses on the **reliability and authenticity** of a document rather than the medium in which it exists. A document does not become legally inferior simply because it is stored on a server instead of in a filing cabinet.

## Electronic Records as Evidence

As more and more communication, business transactions, and government records moved online, courts had to answer an important question:

### Can an electronic record be used as evidence in the same way as a paper document?

The answer is yes—but only if certain legal requirements are met.

While Section 4 of the Information Technology Act gives legal recognition to electronic records, their admissibility in court is governed primarily by the **Indian Evidence Act, 1872** (now substantially carried forward under the **Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**). The IT Act and the law of evidence therefore work together: the IT Act recognises electronic records as legally valid, while the Evidence Act lays down the conditions for proving them in court.

### What is an electronic record?

As discussed earlier, an electronic record includes any information created, stored, received, or transmitted electronically. This covers emails, text messages, PDFs, photographs, CCTV footage, WhatsApp chats, databases, audio recordings, videos, and many other forms of digital information.

Today, electronic records appear in almost every legal dispute—from commercial litigation and contract disputes to criminal investigations and family law matters.

### The challenge with electronic evidence

Unlike a paper document, an electronic record can be copied, edited, or deleted without leaving obvious signs of alteration. A photograph can be edited, an email can be manipulated, and a document can be modified in seconds.

Because digital evidence is easier to tamper with, the law requires additional safeguards before it is admitted in court. The purpose of these safeguards is not to make electronic evidence difficult to use, but to ensure that the record being produced is genuine and has not been altered.

### Sections 65A and 65B of the Evidence Act

The special procedure for proving electronic records is contained in **Sections 65A and 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872**.

Section 65A acts as the gateway provision. It states that the contents of an electronic record must be proved in accordance with Section 65B.

Section 65B then explains when a copy of an electronic record—such as a printout, CD, DVD, pen drive, or other computer output—can be treated as a document and admitted as evidence.

In other words, unlike ordinary documents, electronic records cannot usually be proved using the general rules of documentary evidence. They must satisfy the specific requirements laid down in Section 65B.

### The Section 65B Certificate

One of the most important requirements under Section 65B is the certificate mentioned in Section 65B(4).

This certificate serves as proof that the electronic record is genuine. It must:

- identify the electronic record being produced;
- explain how it was created or obtained;
- describe the computer or device from which it was generated; and
- be signed by a person occupying a responsible position, such as an IT administrator, system manager, or another person familiar with the operation of the device.

The certificate essentially assures the court that the electronic record has been produced from a reliable source and has not been altered during the process.

Without this safeguard, it would often be difficult to distinguish genuine electronic evidence from manipulated or fabricated material.

<Example title="Practical examples">

The Section 65B certificate may be required for many forms of electronic evidence, including:

- emails;

- WhatsApp conversations;

- SMS messages;

- CCTV footage;

- digital photographs;

- audio and video recordings;

- call detail records; and

- social media posts.

Where necessary, forensic experts may also compare the **hash values** of electronic files. Since even a minor change to a file produces a different hash value, matching hashes help demonstrate that the record has remained unchanged.

</Example>

<CaseLaw title="Judicial interpretation">

The law on electronic evidence has been shaped by several important Supreme Court decisions.

</CaseLaw>

### _Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer_ (2014)

This case fundamentally changed the law on electronic evidence in India.

The dispute involved electronic evidence stored on CDs that had been produced in court without the certificate required under Section 65B.

The Supreme Court held that because electronic records are particularly vulnerable to alteration and manipulation, the safeguards contained in Section 65B are mandatory. As a result, electronic records that do not satisfy these requirements are generally not admissible as evidence.

This decision established Section 65B as the primary method of proving electronic records before Indian courts.

### _Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal_ (2020)

The Supreme Court later clarified that the requirement of a Section 65B certificate remains mandatory in most cases.

However, the Court also recognised that a party may not always have control over the device containing the electronic record. For example, CCTV footage may be held by a shopping mall, or call records may be in the custody of a telecom service provider.

In such situations, where the party cannot reasonably obtain the certificate themselves, the courts may adopt a more practical approach to ensure that justice is not defeated.

### _Sonu @ Amar v. State of Haryana_ (2017)

The Supreme Court held that objections relating to the absence of a Section 65B certificate should ordinarily be raised when the evidence is first produced. A party should not wait until the appeal stage to challenge its admissibility on this ground.

### _Union of India v. Ravindra Desai_ (2018)

The Court also recognised that, in appropriate cases, the absence of a Section 65B certificate may be treated as a curable defect. This means that the certificate can sometimes be produced later, depending on the circumstances of the case.

### Why these provisions matter

Today, a significant portion of the evidence produced before courts exists only in digital form. Commercial contracts are negotiated through email, conversations take place on messaging applications, payments are made electronically, and surveillance cameras record important events.

Sections 65A and 65B ensure that courts can rely on these electronic records while protecting against fabrication and manipulation. Together, they strike a balance between embracing digital technology and preserving the integrity of the judicial process.

## E-Contracts: Validity and Enforcement

Today, most contracts are entered into electronically rather than on paper. Whether you're opening a bank account online, subscribing to a streaming service, buying something on Amazon, or downloading an app, you're almost certainly entering into an electronic contract (or **e-contract**).

But this raises an obvious question:

### Can a contract be legally valid even if no physical document is signed?

The answer is **yes**.

Section 10A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 makes it clear that a contract cannot be denied legal validity simply because it was formed through electronic means.

This provision works alongside the **Indian Contract Act, 1872**. An electronic contract must satisfy the same essential requirements as any other contract. In other words, it must contain:

- an offer;
- acceptance;
- lawful consideration;
- free consent;
- competent parties; and
- a lawful object.

The only difference is that these requirements are fulfilled electronically instead of through paper documents.

### How are e-contracts authenticated?

Some electronic contracts are authenticated using **digital signatures** or other forms of electronic signatures recognised under Sections 3 and 3A of the IT Act.

However, many everyday online contracts do not require formal digital signatures. Instead, they rely on the user's conduct—for example, clicking a button, creating an account, or continuing to use a service after being presented with its terms.

Whether such contracts are enforceable depends largely on whether the user had a reasonable opportunity to read and accept the terms.

### Types of Electronic Contracts

Electronic contracts generally fall into two broad categories.

<Definition title="1. Negotiated Electronic Contracts">

These are contracts where the parties negotiate the terms before reaching an agreement.

A common example is businesses negotiating a commercial agreement through email. The parties may exchange several drafts before agreeing on the final terms.

The Supreme Court recognised the validity of such contracts in **Trimex International FZE Ltd. v. Vedanta Aluminium Ltd. (2010)**.

In that case, the parties negotiated the sale of bauxite entirely through email. Although no formal paper contract was ever signed, both parties had agreed on all the essential terms.

The Supreme Court held that a binding contract had been created. The absence of a physical document did not make the agreement invalid.

The decision confirms that what matters is **whether the parties reached a genuine agreement—not whether they signed a paper document.**

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Standard Form Contracts">

Most online contracts are **standard form contracts**.

Instead of negotiating individual terms with every customer, businesses prepare one standard agreement that applies to everyone.

Users can either accept the terms or refuse to use the service. There is usually no opportunity to negotiate individual clauses.

For this reason, these contracts are often described as **"take it or leave it" agreements.**

The three most common types are discussed below.

### Click-Wrap Agreements

A click-wrap agreement requires the user to actively click a button such as **"I Agree"**, **"Accept"**, or **"Continue"** before accessing a service.

Because the user performs a clear, positive act indicating acceptance, courts generally consider click-wrap agreements to be enforceable.

<Example title="Example">

Before creating an account on a website, you're asked to tick a box confirming that you accept the Terms of Service.

By clicking **"I Agree,"** you are expressly accepting the contract.

</Example>

<CaseLaw title="Judicial interpretation">

In **Amazon Seller Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Rajan Arora (2021)**, the Delhi High Court held that clicking **"I Agree"** amounted to valid acceptance of the agreement, including its arbitration clause.

Similarly, although it did not involve an electronic contract, the Supreme Court in **LIC of India v. Consumer Education & Research Centre (1995)** observed that standard form contracts are generally enforceable unless their terms are unfair, unreasonable, or unconscionable.

</CaseLaw>

### Browse-Wrap Agreements

Browse-wrap agreements work differently.

The website does not ask the user to expressly accept the terms. Instead, it assumes that by continuing to browse or use the website, the user has accepted them.

The terms are often available through a small hyperlink at the bottom of the webpage.

Because users may never actually see these terms, courts examine browse-wrap agreements much more carefully.

<Example title="Example">

A website states:

"By continuing to use this website, you agree to our Terms and Conditions."

If the user is never required to click "I Agree," it may be difficult to prove that they actually accepted those terms.

</Example>

<CaseLaw title="Judicial interpretation">

In **Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble Inc. (2014)**, a persuasive decision from the United States, the Court refused to enforce a browse-wrap agreement because the terms were not prominently displayed and the user had not been given adequate notice of them.

Although this is not binding in India, Indian courts have generally adopted a similar approach.

In **Ravi Prakash Goel v. Chandra Prakash Goel (2008)**, the Supreme Court emphasised that a contract requires knowledge of and consent to its terms. This principle suggests that hidden or inconspicuous online terms may not always be enforceable.

</CaseLaw>

### Shrink-Wrap Agreements

Shrink-wrap agreements are commonly used for packaged software.

The contract is usually contained inside the product packaging, and opening the package or installing the software is treated as acceptance of the terms.

Although Indian courts have not extensively examined shrink-wrap agreements, they are likely to apply the same principles used for other electronic contracts.

The key question will usually be whether the user had a reasonable opportunity to read the terms before becoming bound by them.

### When will a court refuse to enforce an e-contract?

Courts do not automatically enforce every electronic contract.

Like any other agreement, an e-contract may be declared unenforceable if:

- the parties did not genuinely consent;
- important terms were hidden or misleading;
- the contract is unfair or unconscionable; or
- the essential requirements of a valid contract are not satisfied.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasised that every valid contract requires a genuine **meeting of minds** between the parties.

In **Shakti Bhog Foods Ltd. v. Kola Shipping Ltd. (2009)**, the Court observed that a contract cannot exist unless both parties have actually agreed to its essential terms.

Similarly, in **Pee Aar Securities Ltd. v. SEBI (2013)**, the Supreme Court held that unfair or unreasonable clauses in standard form contracts may be struck down, particularly where one party has significantly greater bargaining power than the other.

### E-Contracts as Evidence

Like any other electronic record, an electronic contract may need to be produced before a court.

Where the contract exists only in electronic form—for example, as an email exchange or PDF—it must generally comply with the requirements of **Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act** before it can be admitted as evidence.

As discussed in the previous section, this usually requires a valid Section 65B certificate unless an exception recognised by the courts applies.

### Why this matters

Electronic contracts have become the foundation of the digital economy. Every day, individuals and businesses enter into legally binding agreements without ever signing a physical document.

Section 10A of the IT Act ensures that these contracts are treated no differently from traditional paper agreements. At the same time, the general principles of contract law continue to protect parties from unfair terms, lack of consent, and other defects that could make an agreement unenforceable.

</Definition>
## Part II: Regulation of Digital Infrastructure

In the previous chapter, we saw how the Information Technology Act gives legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures.

But that raises another important question.

### How can someone trust that a digital signature actually belongs to the person who claims to have signed the document?

For example, suppose you receive a digitally signed contract from a company. How do you know that the digital signature genuinely belongs to that company and has not been forged?

The answer lies in a system known as **Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)**.

The IT Act creates an entire legal framework to regulate this system. It identifies who can issue Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs), who supervises them, and how digital signatures are verified. Together, these institutions create a chain of trust that allows individuals, businesses, and government agencies to rely on electronic documents with confidence.

## Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA)

The **Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA)** is the regulator responsible for India's digital signature ecosystem.

The Central Government appoints the CCA under **Section 17** of the IT Act.

Its primary role is to regulate organisations known as **Certifying Authorities (CAs)**, which are licensed to issue Digital Signature Certificates.

Think of the CCA as the regulator of digital identity providers. Just as a financial regulator supervises banks, the CCA supervises organisations that verify digital identities and issue Digital Signature Certificates.

### Licensing Certifying Authorities

A company or organisation cannot begin issuing Digital Signature Certificates simply because it has the necessary technology.

Instead, it must first obtain a licence from the CCA under **Section 21** of the IT Act.

Although the Act states that **"any person"** may apply for a licence, approval is granted only if the applicant satisfies the technical, financial, operational, and security standards prescribed under the Act and the relevant rules.

Before granting a licence, the CCA examines factors such as:

- technical expertise;
- security infrastructure;
- financial capability;
- qualified personnel; and
- compliance with the prescribed operational standards.

Only organisations that satisfy these requirements become licensed **Certifying Authorities (CAs).**

### Supervising Certifying Authorities

The CCA's role does not end once a licence is granted.

It continues to supervise Certifying Authorities to ensure that they comply with the IT Act and the standards prescribed under it.

If a Certifying Authority fails to meet these requirements, the CCA may suspend or revoke its licence under **Sections 25 and 26**, after giving it an opportunity to be heard.

For example, the CCA may take action where:

- important information was concealed while applying for the licence;
- the Certifying Authority no longer satisfies the prescribed standards;
- it fails to comply with the Controller's directions; or
- its operations create security risks.

If a licence is suspended or revoked, the CCA must publish an appropriate notice so that users are aware that the organisation is no longer authorised to issue Digital Signature Certificates.

### When can a licence be refused?

The **Information Technology (Certifying Authorities) Rules, 2000** also specify circumstances in which the CCA may refuse to grant a licence.

For example, an application may be rejected if the applicant:

- fails to provide the required information;
- is being wound up or placed under the control of a court-appointed receiver;
- has been convicted of offences involving fraud or dishonesty;
- fails to furnish the required bank guarantee;
- does not comply with its Certification Practice Statement;
- fails to submit mandatory audit reports; or
- is otherwise unable to satisfy the Controller that it can safely perform the functions of a Certifying Authority.

These safeguards help ensure that only reliable organisations are entrusted with issuing Digital Signature Certificates.

## Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)

The legal framework described above operates through **Public Key Infrastructure (PKI).**

PKI is the technical system that allows digital signatures to be trusted.

Earlier, we saw that digital signatures rely on **public keys** and **private keys**.

PKI builds on that idea by creating a chain of trust between everyone involved in the system.

Instead of asking every user to verify everyone else's identity individually, trusted authorities verify identities on behalf of everyone else.

This makes secure electronic communication possible on a national scale.

### The Chain of Trust

India's Public Key Infrastructure works in layers.

At the top of the system is the **Root Certifying Authority of India (RCAI)**.

Below it are the licensed **Certifying Authorities (CAs)**.

Finally, at the bottom are the **subscribers**—the individuals, companies, and organisations that obtain Digital Signature Certificates.

Each level verifies the one below it.

As a result, anyone who trusts the Root Certifying Authority can ultimately trust the Digital Signature Certificate issued to an individual subscriber.

This layered structure is commonly referred to as the **chain of trust**.

### How Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) Works

As we saw earlier, every digital signature relies on a **public key** and a **private key**. But this creates an important problem.

### How do you know that a public key actually belongs to the person or organisation claiming to own it?

For example, imagine you receive a digitally signed contract from a company. The signature can only be verified using the company's public key.

But what if someone creates a fake public key and falsely claims that it belongs to the company?

If there were no way to verify ownership, digital signatures would quickly become unreliable.

This is exactly the problem that **Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)** solves.

Instead of asking every person to verify every public key themselves, PKI relies on trusted organisations that verify identities and issue Digital Signature Certificates. Anyone who trusts these organisations can also trust the certificates they issue.

### The Chain of Trust

India's PKI follows a simple hierarchy where each level verifies the identity of the one below it.

As a result, when you verify someone's Digital Signature Certificate, you are not just trusting that individual—you are trusting the entire chain that issued the certificate.

### Step 1: The Root Certifying Authority (RCAI)

At the top of India's PKI is the **Root Certifying Authority of India (RCAI).**

The RCAI is the highest level of trust in the system.

It does **not** issue Digital Signature Certificates directly to individuals or companies.

Instead, its role is to certify the organisations that are authorised to issue those certificates.

These organisations are called **Certifying Authorities (CAs).**

Once the Controller of Certifying Authorities grants a licence under Section 21, the RCAI digitally signs the Certifying Authority's public key.

This tells everyone:

"This Certifying Authority has been verified and can be trusted."

### Step 2: Certifying Authorities

After becoming licensed, a Certifying Authority can begin issuing **Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs)** to subscribers.

These subscribers may include:

- individuals;
- companies;
- government departments; or
- other organisations.

Before issuing a certificate, the Certifying Authority must verify the applicant's identity.

Depending on the type of certificate, this may involve verifying documents such as:

- Aadhaar;
- PAN;
- Passport;
- organisational credentials; or
- other forms of identity prescribed by the Controller.

Some categories of certificates may also require biometric or video-based verification.

Only after completing this verification process can the Certifying Authority issue a Digital Signature Certificate.

### Step 3: Creating the Digital Signature Certificate

Once the subscriber's identity has been verified, a **pair of cryptographic keys** is generated.

These consist of:

- a **private key**, which remains secret and is known only to the subscriber; and
- a **public key**, which forms part of the Digital Signature Certificate.

The private key is usually stored securely in a hardware token or other protected environment so that nobody else can access it.

The public key, on the other hand, is meant to be shared with anyone who wishes to verify the subscriber's digital signature.

### What does the Digital Signature Certificate contain?

A Digital Signature Certificate contains much more than just the subscriber's public key.

It generally includes:

- the subscriber's public key;
- the subscriber's identity;
- the certificate's serial number;
- its validity period;
- the name of the issuing Certifying Authority;
- the hashing algorithm used; and
- the digital signature of the Certifying Authority.

Together, this information allows anyone receiving the certificate to verify both the subscriber's identity and the authenticity of the certificate itself.

### Why doesn't the Certifying Authority sign the public key directly?

This is where hashing comes into the picture.

Instead of digitally signing the entire certificate, the Certifying Authority first runs the certificate through a **hash function**.

As discussed earlier, a hash function converts data into a fixed-length value known as a **hash** or **digital fingerprint**.

Even changing a single character in the certificate would produce an entirely different hash.

The Certifying Authority then encrypts this hash using **its own private key**.

The encrypted hash becomes the Certifying Authority's digital signature on the certificate.

This approach is much faster and more efficient than encrypting the entire certificate, while providing the same assurance that the certificate has not been altered.

### How is the certificate verified?

Suppose someone receives a digitally signed document.

Before trusting the signature, they first verify the Digital Signature Certificate.

This happens in four steps.

**Step 1:** The verifier obtains the issuing Certifying Authority's public key.

**Step 2:** Using that public key, the verifier decrypts the Certifying Authority's digital signature attached to the certificate.

**Step 3:** The verifier independently generates a fresh hash of the certificate using the same hashing algorithm.

**Step 4:** The two hash values are compared.

If the hashes match, the verifier knows that:

- the certificate has not been altered;
- it was genuinely issued by the Certifying Authority; and
- the subscriber's public key can be trusted.

If the hashes do not match, the certificate has either been tampered with or is not genuine.

### Why does the system work?

The entire system depends on **trust flowing down the chain**.

You trust the **Root Certifying Authority of India**.

Because the RCAI trusts a licensed **Certifying Authority**, you trust that Certifying Authority.

Because the Certifying Authority has verified the subscriber's identity before issuing the certificate, you can trust the subscriber's Digital Signature Certificate.

This layered verification process allows millions of people to exchange digitally signed documents without having to verify each other's identities individually.

### Why PKI matters

Every time you digitally sign an income tax return, submit a filing to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, participate in an e-procurement process, or execute a high-value electronic contract, Public Key Infrastructure is working behind the scenes.

Without PKI, there would be no reliable way to prove who signed an electronic document or whether it had been altered after it was signed.

The IT Act therefore combines cryptography with legal regulation to create a trusted digital ecosystem. By regulating Certifying Authorities and establishing a secure chain of trust, it ensures that digital signatures remain both technically reliable and legally enforceable.

## Issuance, Suspension and Revocation of Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs)

A Digital Signature Certificate (DSC) is not permanent. Like a passport or driving licence, it can be **issued**, **suspended**, or **revoked** under certain circumstances.

The Information Technology Act lays down clear rules governing each stage of a certificate's lifecycle. These safeguards help ensure that Digital Signature Certificates remain trustworthy and cannot continue to be used if their authenticity or security is compromised.

### Issuing a Digital Signature Certificate

As discussed earlier, a person or organisation seeking a Digital Signature Certificate must apply to a licensed **Certifying Authority (CA)** under **Section 35** of the IT Act.

Before issuing the certificate, the Certifying Authority must verify the applicant's identity and ensure that all legal and technical requirements have been satisfied.

Once the verification process is complete, the Certifying Authority issues the Digital Signature Certificate, linking the subscriber's verified identity with their public key.

The subscriber can then use the corresponding private key to digitally sign electronic records.

### Suspension of a Digital Signature Certificate

There may be situations where a Digital Signature Certificate should temporarily stop being used while an issue is investigated. In such cases, the Certifying Authority may **suspend** the certificate.

Under the IT Act, a Digital Signature Certificate may be suspended in two situations:

- at the request of the subscriber (or someone authorised to act on their behalf); or
- where the Certifying Authority considers suspension necessary in the public interest.

Suspension is generally intended to be a temporary measure. For example, a subscriber may request suspension if they believe their private key has been compromised or their hardware token has been lost.

### Opportunity to be heard

The Act also provides an important safeguard.

If a suspension is likely to continue for **more than 15 days**, the subscriber must first be given an opportunity to be heard.

This ensures that certificates are not suspended for extended periods without following principles of natural justice.

### Revocation of a Digital Signature Certificate

Revocation is different from suspension.

While suspension is temporary, **revocation permanently cancels the certificate**, meaning it can no longer be used.

A Certifying Authority may revoke a Digital Signature Certificate in several circumstances.

### At the subscriber's request

A certificate may be revoked if:

- the subscriber requests its cancellation; or
- an authorised representative makes the request on the subscriber's behalf.

### Where the subscriber no longer exists

A certificate may also be revoked if:

- the subscriber has died; or
- the subscriber is a company, firm, or other organisation that has been dissolved, wound up, or otherwise ceased to exist.

Since the subscriber no longer exists, there is no longer any purpose in keeping the certificate active.

### Where the certificate should never have remained valid

The Certifying Authority may also revoke a certificate where serious problems affect its validity, including where:

- important facts were concealed or misrepresented while obtaining the certificate;
- the legal requirements for issuing the certificate were never satisfied;
- the Certifying Authority's own private key or security system has been compromised; or
- the subscriber has been declared insolvent or otherwise becomes legally incapable of continuing to hold the certificate.

In each of these situations, allowing the certificate to remain active could undermine the reliability of the digital signature system.

### Opportunity to be heard

Before revoking a Digital Signature Certificate, the subscriber must generally be given an opportunity to present their case.

This reflects the broader principle of **natural justice**, ensuring that a person's digital identity cannot be cancelled without following a fair procedure.

### Notice of suspension or revocation

Whenever a Digital Signature Certificate is suspended or revoked, the Certifying Authority must:

- notify the subscriber; and
- publish the suspension or revocation in the certificate repository referred to in the certificate.

This allows anyone relying on the certificate to verify whether it is still valid before accepting a digitally signed document.

### X.509 Certificate Policy for India

The IT Act provides the legal framework for Digital Signature Certificates, but the detailed technical standards governing their use are contained in the **X.509 Certificate Policy for India**.

This policy lays down the operational and security requirements that licensed Certifying Authorities must follow. Among other things, it governs:

- the issuance and management of Digital Signature Certificates;
- identity verification procedures;
- security standards for key management;
- certificate validity and renewal; and
- the circumstances in which certificates may be suspended or revoked.

Together with the IT Act and the Information Technology (Certifying Authorities) Rules, the X.509 Certificate Policy forms the technical backbone of India's Public Key Infrastructure.

### Why these provisions matter

The reliability of digital signatures depends on more than just cryptography—it also depends on trust.

If compromised or invalid certificates could continue to be used indefinitely, it would become much easier to impersonate individuals, forge electronic documents, and undermine confidence in digital transactions.

By regulating how Digital Signature Certificates are issued, suspended, and revoked, the IT Act ensures that only valid and trustworthy certificates remain in circulation. This is essential for maintaining confidence in electronic contracts, e-governance, digital filings, and countless other online transactions that rely on digital signatures.

## Part III: Cybersecurity and Privacy

As technology has become more deeply integrated into everyday life, so have the risks associated with it. Today, individuals and organisations routinely store sensitive information—such as financial records, medical information, business data, and personal communications—in digital form.

While this has made information easier to access and share, it has also made it easier to steal, manipulate, or destroy.

Cybercriminals can hack into computer systems, steal confidential information, install malicious software, disrupt online services, or impersonate individuals for financial gain. In many cases, these attacks occur without the knowledge of the person whose data has been compromised.

The Information Technology Act addresses these risks through a combination of **preventive measures**, **civil liability**, **criminal offences**, and **institutional mechanisms** for responding to cybersecurity incidents.

### What is Cybersecurity?

Section **2(1)(nb)** of the IT Act defines **cybersecurity** as the protection of:

- information;
- computers;
- computer resources;
- communication devices;
- networks; and
- the information stored on them,

from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.

Put simply, cybersecurity is about ensuring that digital systems remain **secure, reliable, and available only to authorised users.**

Although the Act does not define the term **cybercrime**, most cyber offences involve the unauthorised use of a computer system or computer resource to access, manipulate, damage, or steal digital information.

<CaseLaw title="Judicial Interpretation">

The Gujarat High Court discussed the scope of cyber offences in **Jaydeep Vrujlal Depani v. State of Gujarat**.

The Court observed that cyber offences include crimes committed against individuals or groups using computers, mobile phones, or the internet with the intention of causing financial loss, reputational harm, or physical or mental injury.

These offences may involve websites, email, messaging platforms, online forums, social media, or other forms of digital communication.

The decision reflects the broad approach adopted by the IT Act, recognising that cybercrime extends far beyond traditional hacking.

</CaseLaw>

### How does the IT Act regulate cybersecurity?

The IT Act adopts a three-layered approach to cybersecurity.

<Definition title="1. Prevention">

The Act requires organisations handling sensitive information to adopt appropriate security practices and procedures to reduce the risk of data breaches and cyberattacks.

These obligations are primarily found in **Section 43A** and the **Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011**, commonly known as the **SPDI Rules**.

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Punishment">

The Act imposes both **civil liability** and **criminal liability** for cyber offences.

Civil provisions generally require wrongdoers to compensate victims for the loss they have caused.

Criminal provisions apply where the conduct is sufficiently serious—for example, where there is dishonest or fraudulent intent, identity theft, cyber fraud, privacy violations, cyber terrorism, or the publication of unlawful online content.

We will examine these offences in detail later in this chapter.

</Definition>

<Definition title="3. Incident Response">

Even the strongest security systems cannot prevent every cyberattack.

For this reason, the IT Act also establishes **CERT-In** (the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team) as India's national agency for responding to cybersecurity incidents.

CERT-In monitors cyber threats, issues security advisories, coordinates responses to major incidents, and helps organisations recover from cyberattacks.

Together, these three components—**prevention, punishment, and incident response**—form the foundation of India's cybersecurity framework under the IT Act.

### Information Security Standards

Preventing cyberattacks is often more effective than responding to them after they occur.

Recognising this, the IT Act does not simply punish organisations after a data breach. It also requires certain organisations to implement reasonable security measures to protect the information they hold.

These obligations are contained in **Section 43A** of the IT Act and the **Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011**, commonly known as the **SPDI Rules**.

Section 43A was one of the most significant additions introduced by the **Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008**. For the first time, it imposed a statutory obligation on companies to protect certain categories of personal information and made them financially liable where their failure to implement reasonable security practices caused harm.

The SPDI Rules were later introduced to explain what these obligations actually require in practice.

</Definition>

## Section 43A – Liability for Failure to Protect Data

Section 43A applies specifically to **body corporates**, such as companies and other organisations that collect or process personal information.

It provides that a body corporate may be liable to pay compensation if three conditions are satisfied:

- it possesses, deals with, or handles **Sensitive Personal Data or Information (SPDI)**;
- the information is handled through a computer resource that the organisation owns, controls, or operates; and
- a person's wrongful loss or another person's wrongful gain results from the organisation's failure to implement **reasonable security practices and procedures**.

In simple terms, Section 43A says that organisations cannot ignore cybersecurity. If they fail to take reasonable steps to protect sensitive information and that failure causes harm, they may be required to compensate the affected person.

### Why were the SPDI Rules introduced?

Section 43A established the obligation to protect sensitive information, but it left several important questions unanswered.

For example:

- What exactly counts as **Sensitive Personal Data or Information**?
- What are **reasonable security practices**?
- Does every data breach automatically mean that an organisation failed to provide adequate security?

The SPDI Rules answer these questions by setting out the standards that organisations are expected to follow when collecting, storing, using, and sharing sensitive personal information.

They effectively translate the broad obligations contained in Section 43A into practical compliance requirements.

### Why this matters

Section 43A and the SPDI Rules represented India's first comprehensive attempt to regulate how private organisations protect personal information.

Although many aspects of data protection are now governed by the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, the SPDI Rules remain an important part of the IT Act and continue to apply in contexts where they have not been displaced. They also provide valuable insight into how Indian data protection law evolved before the enactment of a dedicated privacy statute.

### The SPDI Rules, 2011

The **Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011**, commonly known as the **SPDI Rules**, were introduced to give practical effect to Section 43A of the IT Act.

While Section 43A establishes that organisations can be held liable for failing to protect sensitive personal information, the SPDI Rules explain **what information is protected and what organisations must do to protect it.**

Although India now has the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)**, the SPDI Rules remain an important part of India's digital law framework and continue to illustrate the country's earlier approach to data protection.

### What is Sensitive Personal Data or Information (SPDI)?

The SPDI Rules distinguish between **personal information** and **Sensitive Personal Data or Information (SPDI).**

### Personal Information

Under Rule 2(1)(i), **personal information** means any information relating to a natural person that can identify that person, either by itself or when combined with other information that is reasonably available.

For example, information such as a person's name, address, email address, or phone number may qualify as personal information if it can be linked to an identifiable individual.

### Sensitive Personal Data or Information (SPDI)

Some categories of personal information are considered especially sensitive because unauthorised access to them could cause significant harm.

Rule 3 classifies the following as **Sensitive Personal Data or Information (SPDI):**

- passwords;
- financial information, such as bank account or payment card details;
- physical, physiological, and mental health information;
- medical records and medical history;
- biometric information; and
- any information relating to these categories that is provided to a body corporate for providing a service.

These categories receive greater legal protection because they are more likely to expose individuals to financial loss, identity theft, discrimination, or other forms of harm if they are compromised.

It is important to remember that these obligations apply only to **body corporates**. The SPDI Rules do not impose the same compliance requirements on individuals acting in a personal capacity.

### Collecting SPDI

The SPDI Rules do not prohibit organisations from collecting sensitive information. Instead, they regulate **how** it may be collected.

The basic principle is simple:

### Collect only what you genuinely need, and tell people why you are collecting it.

<Definition title="1. Obtain informed consent">

Before collecting SPDI, a body corporate must clearly explain:

- what information it is collecting;
- why it is collecting the information;
- how it will use the information;
- how long it will retain the information; and
- whether the information may be shared with anyone else.

This information should be provided through an accessible privacy policy that is easy for individuals to understand.

Only after providing this information should the organisation obtain the individual's consent.

The objective is to ensure that consent is informed rather than merely procedural.

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Collect only what is necessary">

Rule 5 also incorporates the principle of **data minimisation**.

An organisation should collect SPDI only if:

- the collection is for a lawful purpose connected with its functions or activities; and
- the information is genuinely necessary for that purpose.

Simply because an organisation can collect certain information does not mean it should.

For example, an online food delivery platform may reasonably require your delivery address, but it would be difficult to justify collecting your medical records simply to deliver a meal.

</Definition>
### Retaining and Using SPDI

Collecting information is only the beginning. Organisations must also ensure that they use and retain it responsibly. The SPDI Rules create a framework for that as well:

<Definition title="1. Do not retain data longer than necessary">

Rule 5(4) requires organisations to retain SPDI only for as long as it is needed for the purpose for which it was collected, unless another law requires a longer retention period.

Once that purpose has been fulfilled, the information should not continue to be stored indefinitely.

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Use the data only for its intended purpose">

Rule 5(5) states that SPDI should be used only for the purpose for which it was collected.

For example, if a customer provides financial information to complete a payment, that information should not later be used for unrelated purposes without an appropriate legal basis.

This reflects the broader principle of **purpose limitation**, which remains a cornerstone of modern data protection law.

</Definition>

<Definition title="3. Right to withdraw consent">

Individuals are not locked into their original decision.

Under Rule 5(7), they may withdraw their consent at any time by giving written notice.

However, if the information is essential for providing a particular service, the organisation may refuse to continue providing that service after consent has been withdrawn.

For example, if an online payment service requires bank account information to process transactions, withdrawing consent to process that information may make it impossible for the service to operate.

</Definition>

<Definition title="4. Right to review and correct information">

Rule 5(6) gives individuals the right to review the personal information held about them and request corrections where it is inaccurate.

The individual, however, remains responsible for ensuring that the corrected information is accurate.

</Definition>

### Sharing SPDI

The SPDI Rules also regulate when sensitive information may be shared with others.

<Definition title="1. Sharing with private organisations">

As a general rule, SPDI cannot be shared with another organisation without the individual's prior permission.

That permission may be given in writing, by email, or through another recognised form of consent.

</Definition>

<Definition title="2. Sharing with Government agencies">

An important exception applies to Government agencies.

Consent is **not required** where a Government agency is legally authorised to obtain the information for purposes such as:

- identity verification;
- preventing or detecting offences;
- investigating cyber incidents;
- prosecution; or
- enforcing the law.

However, the agency must make a formal request explaining:

- what information is required; and
- the legal purpose for which it is being sought.

This helps ensure that access to sensitive information is not exercised arbitrarily.

</Definition>

<Definition title="3. Cross-Border Data Transfers">

Rule 7 permits organisations to transfer SPDI to another organisation, whether located in India or abroad, provided that two conditions are satisfied.

First, the transfer must be **necessary** for performing a lawful contract between the organisation and the individual.

Second, the receiving organisation must provide **the same level of data protection** that the transferring organisation is required to maintain under the SPDI Rules.

This was India's first statutory attempt to regulate international data transfers—an issue that later became central to the DPDP Act.

</Definition>

### Reasonable Security Practices

Perhaps the most important obligation under the SPDI Rules is the requirement to implement **reasonable security practices and procedures**.

Rule 8 recognises **ISO/IEC 27001**, an internationally accepted information security standard, as one recognised benchmark for compliance.

Organisations are expected to implement appropriate:

- technical safeguards;
- managerial safeguards; and
- organisational policies

to protect the information they handle.

The exact measures required will depend on the nature of the organisation and the risks associated with its activities.

### Alternative security standards

Some industries may follow security standards other than ISO/IEC 27001.

The SPDI Rules permit this, provided those standards receive approval from the Central Government.

This allows the law to remain flexible as cybersecurity standards continue to evolve.

### Regular security audits

Compliance is not a one-time exercise.

Rule 8 requires organisations to conduct security audits:

- at least once every year; or
- whenever significant changes are made to their systems or security infrastructure.

Regular audits help organisations identify vulnerabilities before they result in data breaches or cybersecurity incidents.

### Why these Rules matter

The SPDI Rules introduced many concepts that are now considered fundamental to modern privacy law, including:

- informed consent;
- purpose limitation;
- data minimisation;
- storage limitation;
- correction rights;
- security safeguards; and
- accountability for organisations handling sensitive personal information.

Although many of these concepts have since been expanded and modernised under the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023**, the SPDI Rules marked India's first comprehensive attempt to regulate how private organisations collect, use, store, and protect personal information.

## Punitive Measures under the IT Act

The IT Act does more than recognise electronic records and regulate digital infrastructure. It also creates a legal framework for dealing with cyber offences.

Broadly speaking, the Act provides **two kinds of consequences** when someone misuses a computer or computer system:

- **Civil liability**, where the wrongdoer must compensate the victim for the loss caused; and
- **Criminal liability**, where the wrongdoer may be prosecuted and punished with imprisonment, a fine, or both.

The distinction is important because not every cybersecurity incident is a crime. Some acts merely require the wrongdoer to compensate the victim, while others are considered serious enough to attract criminal punishment.

In some situations, **both civil and criminal proceedings may arise from the same incident.**

### Civil Liability – Section 43

Section 43 is the primary civil liability provision under the IT Act.

It provides that a person who accesses or interferes with another person's computer, computer system, computer network, or computer resource **without permission**, and causes loss or damage, may be required to pay compensation to the affected person.

Unlike many criminal provisions, **Section 43 does not require dishonest or fraudulent intent.** The focus is on the unauthorised act and the harm it causes.

### What kinds of activities are covered?

Section 43 covers a wide range of unauthorised activities, including:

- accessing a computer or computer network without permission;
- downloading, copying, or extracting data without authorisation;
- introducing malware, viruses, or other harmful software;
- damaging or disrupting computer systems or networks;
- denying authorised users access to computer resources;
- assisting another person in gaining unauthorised access;
- manipulating computer systems to wrongfully charge another person for services;
- destroying, deleting, or altering data; and
- stealing, concealing, or modifying computer source code where such acts cause loss or damage.

The provision is deliberately broad so that it can address many different forms of unauthorised interference with computer systems.

### What does "without permission" mean?

The IT Act does not define what amounts to valid permission.

In practice, unauthorised access generally arises where a person:

- accesses a computer system without the owner's consent;
- exceeds the authority that has been given to them; or
- circumvents technical or contractual restrictions imposed by the owner.

For example, an employee who accesses confidential files beyond the scope of their authorised role may be acting without permission, even though they are otherwise permitted to use the company's computer system.

### What counts as "access"?

The Act defines **access** broadly.

It is not limited to hacking over the internet.

A person may gain unauthorised access by:

- remotely entering a computer system through a network;
- exploiting software vulnerabilities;
- bypassing passwords or other security measures; or
- physically accessing a computer's hardware or storage devices without authorisation.

In other words, both digital and physical methods of gaining unauthorised access may fall within Section 43.

### Why is unauthorised access taken so seriously?

Unauthorised access is often the starting point for more serious cyber offences.

The **Law Commission of England and Wales** identified three major harms associated with unauthorised access:

- it can cause financial loss to the owner of the computer system;
- it often serves as the first step towards more serious cybercrimes; and
- repeated cyberattacks reduce public confidence in digital systems and discourage investment in technology.

These concerns are equally relevant today, particularly as businesses and governments become increasingly dependent on digital infrastructure.

### What amounts to "damage"?

Section 43 adopts a very broad definition of **damage**.

Damage includes:

- destroying data;
- deleting information;
- altering files;
- adding or modifying data;
- rearranging information; or
- damaging computer systems, databases, software, or networks.

The law therefore recognises that digital harm is not limited to physically destroying a computer. Altering or interfering with digital information may be equally harmful.

### Compensation

Originally, the IT Act capped compensation under Section 43 at **₹5 crore**.

However, the **Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008** removed this limit.

Today, the amount of compensation depends on the value of the claim.

Under **Section 46(1A)**:

- claims **up to ₹5 crore** are decided by the Adjudicating Officer appointed under the Act; and
- claims **exceeding ₹5 crore** must be brought before the court having the appropriate pecuniary jurisdiction.

This allows compensation to be assessed according to the actual loss suffered rather than an arbitrary statutory ceiling.

### Civil Liability vs Criminal Liability

Not every unauthorised act amounts to a crime.

A person may accidentally damage another's computer system or exceed authorised access without any intention to deceive or cause wrongful gain.

In such cases, civil liability under Section 43 may be sufficient.

However, where the same acts are carried out **dishonestly or fraudulently**, the IT Act treats them much more seriously.

These situations fall under the criminal provisions beginning with **Section 65**.

The key difference is **intent**.

Civil liability focuses on the damage caused.

Criminal liability focuses on both the damage **and** the offender's state of mind.

## Criminal Offences under the IT Act

The IT Act creates a number of criminal offences for more serious forms of cyber misconduct.

Unlike Section 43, these provisions generally require proof of a particular mental element—such as **knowledge**, **dishonesty**, **fraud**, or **intent**.

Because criminal convictions can result in imprisonment, the prosecution must establish these elements **beyond reasonable doubt**.

### Relationship with other criminal laws

Many cyber offences may also appear to fall under general criminal laws such as the **Indian Penal Code, 1860** (now largely replaced by the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**).

Where this happens, an important legal principle applies:

### A special law overrides a general law when both deal with the same subject matter.

This principle is expressed through the Latin maxim **_generalia specialibus non derogant_**.

Since the IT Act is a specialised law dealing specifically with cyber offences and electronic records, its provisions generally prevail where there is direct overlap.

<CaseLaw title="Judicial interpretation">

In **Sharat Babu Digumarthi v. State (NCT of Delhi)**, the Supreme Court held that where the IT Act specifically criminalises particular conduct involving electronic records or computer systems, its provisions take precedence over the corresponding provisions of the IPC.

However, this does **not** mean that the IPC (or now the BNS) becomes irrelevant whenever technology is involved.

In **Awadhesh Kumar Parasnath Pathak v. State of Maharashtra (2024)**, the Bombay High Court clarified that the IT Act overrides the general criminal law **only where the ingredients of the two offences are substantially the same**.

If the general criminal offence contains additional elements that are not covered by the IT Act, prosecution under the general criminal law may still be possible.

</CaseLaw>
## Section 65 – Tampering with Computer Source Code

Computers and software operate using **source code**—the human-readable instructions that tell a computer how to perform a particular task. In many cases, organisations are legally required to preserve this source code, either because of regulatory requirements or because it forms part of an official computer system.

Section 65 of the IT Act makes it a criminal offence to tamper with such source code where the law requires it to be maintained.

A person commits an offence if they knowingly or intentionally:

- conceal;
- destroy; or
- alter

computer source code that is legally required to be kept or maintained.

The offence is punishable with:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹2 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### Why does this provision exist?

Source code forms the foundation of many critical digital systems. If it is deliberately altered or destroyed, it may become impossible to understand how a system operated or whether it has been manipulated.

For example, imagine a company that is legally required to maintain the source code of software used to process financial transactions. If an employee deliberately deletes or modifies that source code to conceal fraud, investigators may no longer be able to determine how the system originally functioned.

Section 65 seeks to prevent precisely this kind of misconduct by protecting the integrity of legally maintained source code.

### A note on interpretation

Terms such as **"conceals," "destroys,"** and **"alters"** are not exhaustively defined in the IT Act.

As a result, courts interpret these expressions based on their ordinary meaning and the facts of each case. Over time, judicial decisions will continue to shape the precise scope of this provision.

## Section 66 – Computer-Related Offences

Section 66 builds directly on **Section 43**.

As we saw earlier, Section 43 imposes **civil liability** for unauthorised access to, or interference with, a computer system.

Section 66 takes those very same acts and makes them **criminal offences** when they are committed **dishonestly or fraudulently**.

In other words:

### Section 43 tells you what acts are prohibited. Section 66 tells you when those acts become crimes.

A person who commits any of the acts listed in Section 43 **dishonestly or fraudulently** may be punished with:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹5 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### What do "dishonestly" and "fraudulently" mean?

These terms are not defined in the IT Act itself.

Instead, the Act relies on the definitions found in the **Indian Penal Code, 1860** (now reflected in the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023**).

### Dishonestly

A person acts **dishonestly** when they intend to cause:

- **wrongful gain** to themselves or another person; or
- **wrongful loss** to someone else.

For example, hacking into another person's bank account to transfer money would clearly involve dishonest intent.

### Fraudulently

A person acts **fraudulently** when they act with the intention of deceiving another person.

Fraud generally involves two elements:

- **deception**; and
- **injury or prejudice** caused by that deception.

For example, creating a fake online banking website to steal customers' login credentials would amount to acting fraudulently.

### When does Section 66 apply?

To establish an offence under Section 66, the prosecution must generally prove two things:

1. the accused committed one of the unauthorised acts listed in **Section 43**; and
2. the act was committed **dishonestly or fraudulently**.

Without this additional mental element, the conduct may still attract **civil liability** under Section 43, but it will not ordinarily amount to a criminal offence under Section 66.

### Can a person face both civil and criminal proceedings?

Yes.

A person who commits a computer-related offence may face:

- a civil claim for compensation under **Section 43**; and
- criminal prosecution under **Section 66**.

These proceedings are independent of one another.

This means that compensation awarded in a civil proceeding does not prevent the State from prosecuting the offender for the criminal offence.

Similarly, an acquittal in a criminal case does not automatically prevent a victim from pursuing civil compensation where the legal requirements are otherwise satisfied.

### Why is Section 66 important?

Section 66 is often described as the IT Act's general cybercrime provision because it applies to a wide range of unauthorised activities involving computer systems.

Many cyber offences begin with conduct that falls under Section 43—for example, gaining unauthorised access to a computer or copying confidential information. When those same acts are carried out with dishonest or fraudulent intent, Section 66 transforms what would otherwise be a civil wrong into a criminal offence.

This provision therefore acts as the bridge between civil liability and criminal punishment, making it one of the most frequently invoked sections of the IT Act.

## Section 66A – Sending Offensive Messages (Repealed)

**Important:** Section 66A is **no longer law.** The Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional in **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)**, and it cannot be used to prosecute anyone today.

Despite being repealed, Section 66A remains one of the most significant provisions in the history of India's internet laws because it fundamentally shaped the relationship between free speech and online regulation.

### What did Section 66A provide?

Before it was struck down, Section 66A made it a criminal offence to send electronic messages that were:

- **grossly offensive**;
- **menacing** in nature;
- information known to be false but sent to cause annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred, or ill will; or
- messages sent by email or other electronic communication for the purpose of misleading recipients about their origin.

The offence was punishable with:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine**; or
- **both**.

At first glance, the provision appeared to target online abuse and harmful communications. In practice, however, its wording was so broad that it became a tool for prosecuting a wide range of online speech.

### Why was it controversial?

The biggest criticism of Section 66A was that many of its key expressions were never defined.

Terms such as:

- **"grossly offensive";**
- **"annoyance";**
- **"inconvenience";**
- **"ill will";** and
- **"hatred"**

were vague and highly subjective.

What one person considers offensive may simply be criticism or satire to someone else.

Because the provision did not clearly define its limits, it gave law enforcement authorities wide discretion to decide what kinds of online speech should attract criminal liability.

This created significant uncertainty and raised concerns about freedom of speech.

### How was Section 66A used?

The broad wording of the provision meant that it was invoked in many situations that had little to do with genuine cybercrime.

For example:

- In **Abu v. State of Kerala**, Section 66A was invoked in a case involving allegations of blackmail.
- In **Sushmit Sunilbhai Roy v. State of Gujarat**, it was used in proceedings relating to dowry harassment.
- In **Tintu Shaji v. State of Kerala**, it was invoked in connection with the unauthorised publication of a news excerpt.

These cases illustrated how the provision extended far beyond its original objective and was increasingly being used in disputes involving ordinary online communication.

### _Shreya Singhal v. Union of India_ (2015)

Section 66A ultimately came before the Supreme Court in one of the most important constitutional cases involving the internet.

The case arose after two young women were arrested for Facebook posts questioning the shutdown of Mumbai following the death of political leader **Bal Thackeray**.

Although the criminal proceedings against them were eventually dropped, the incident sparked nationwide debate about the misuse of Section 66A.

This led to a constitutional challenge before the Supreme Court.

### The challenge

The petitioners argued that Section 66A violated **Article 19(1)(a)** of the Constitution, which guarantees the **fundamental right to freedom of speech and expression**.

While Article 19(2) allows the Government to impose reasonable restrictions on free speech—for example, in the interests of national security, public order, defamation, or incitement to an offence—the petitioners argued that Section 66A went far beyond these recognised grounds.

Because the provision criminalised vague concepts such as "annoyance" and "offensive" messages, it could potentially cover a vast amount of legitimate online speech.

This, they argued, would discourage people from expressing their opinions freely—a phenomenon often described as the **"chilling effect"** on free speech.

### The Government's arguments

The Government defended Section 66A by arguing that Parliament has the authority to enact laws addressing new challenges posed by the internet.

It also argued that the possibility of misuse should not, by itself, be a reason to declare a law unconstitutional. According to the Government, misuse could be addressed through better enforcement rather than by striking down the provision altogether.

### The Supreme Court's decision

The Supreme Court unanimously struck down Section 66A as unconstitutional.

The Court held that the provision was **vague, overbroad, and disproportionate**.

In particular, it observed that expressions such as **"grossly offensive"** and **"annoyance"** had no clear legal meaning. As a result, people could not reasonably know what conduct was prohibited.

The Court also found that Section 66A criminalised protected speech without requiring any real connection to the grounds listed in **Article 19(2)**, such as public order, defamation, or incitement to an offence.

Simply causing annoyance or inconvenience could not justify criminal punishment.

Because of its broad wording, the provision had the potential to discourage lawful criticism, satire, political discussion, and other forms of legitimate expression on the internet.

The Court therefore declared Section 66A unconstitutional in its entirety.

### Why _Shreya Singhal_ matters

The decision is widely regarded as one of the most important free speech judgments in Indian constitutional law.

It established several important principles:

- restrictions on online speech are subject to the same constitutional protections as offline speech;
- criminal laws affecting speech must be clear and precisely drafted;
- vague or overbroad laws that discourage lawful expression may violate the Constitution; and
- freedom of speech cannot be restricted merely because someone finds a statement offensive or annoying.

The judgment continues to shape debates on platform regulation, online content moderation, intermediary liability, and digital free speech in India.

### Offences Against Individuals

While Sections 65 and 66 primarily protect computers and computer systems, the next set of offences focuses on protecting **people**.

These provisions deal with situations where technology is used to:

- possess or use stolen devices;
- steal another person's digital identity;
- impersonate someone online; or
- invade an individual's privacy.

Together, they address some of the most common forms of cybercrime encountered today.

## Section 66B – Receiving or Retaining Stolen Computer Resources

Technology has made electronic devices extremely valuable—not just because of the devices themselves, but because of the sensitive information they contain.

A stolen laptop, mobile phone, or hard drive may contain financial records, confidential business information, personal photographs, passwords, or access to online accounts.

Recognising this, Section 66B makes it a criminal offence to **dishonestly receive or retain** a stolen computer resource or communication device when the person knows, or has reason to believe, that it has been stolen.

The offence is punishable with:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹1 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### Why was this provision introduced?

Ordinary theft laws already punish the theft of property.

However, Section 66B addresses a different problem—the circulation and use of **stolen digital devices**.

For example, someone who knowingly purchases a stolen laptop containing confidential company data may not have committed the original theft, but they are still contributing to the misuse of stolen computer resources.

The provision therefore discourages the secondary market for stolen electronic devices and helps protect the sensitive information stored on them.

## Section 66C – Identity Theft

One of the fastest-growing forms of cybercrime is **identity theft**.

Instead of stealing physical property, cybercriminals steal digital credentials that allow them to impersonate another person.

Section 66C makes it an offence to **dishonestly or fraudulently use another person's**:

- electronic signature;
- password; or
- any other unique identification feature.

The punishment is:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹1 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### What is a "unique identification feature"?

The IT Act does not define this expression.

This is intentional.

Rather than limiting the law to existing technologies, Parliament adopted a broad phrase capable of covering new methods of digital identification as technology evolves.

Depending on the circumstances, a unique identification feature may include:

- one-time passwords (OTPs);
- biometric credentials;
- authentication tokens;
- digital certificates;
- Aadhaar-based authentication credentials; or
- other technologies used to uniquely identify an individual.

This flexible approach helps ensure that the provision remains relevant as new authentication technologies emerge.

### Why does identity theft matter?

Many online services rely entirely on digital credentials.

If someone gains access to another person's password, OTP, or digital authentication token, they may be able to:

- access bank accounts;
- make financial transactions;
- impersonate the victim online;
- obtain confidential information; or
- commit further cyber offences.

Section 66C therefore criminalises the misuse of digital identity **even before** it is used to deceive someone else.

## Section 66D – Cheating by Personation Using Computer Resources

Identity theft and impersonation often occur together, but they are not the same.

While Section 66C focuses on **stealing or misusing another person's identity**, Section 66D applies when that stolen identity is actually **used to deceive someone.**

Section 66D makes it a criminal offence to **cheat by personation** using a computer resource or communication device.

The punishment is:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹1 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### What is "cheating by personation"?

The concept comes from the general law of cheating.

A person commits cheating by personation when they pretend to be someone else in order to deceive another person.

This may involve:

- pretending to be a real individual;
- pretending to represent an organisation;
- assuming a false identity; or
- knowingly allowing someone else to be mistaken for another person.

Section 66D applies this traditional concept to the digital world.

<Example title="Common examples">

Section 66D covers many familiar cyber scams, including:

- phishing emails pretending to come from a bank;

- fake customer support calls;

- fraudulent job offers;

- impersonation on social media;

- fake online shopping websites;

- romance scams; and

- investment fraud carried out through digital platforms.

In each of these situations, technology is used to create a false identity and deceive another person.

</Example>

### Section 66C vs Section 66D

Although the two provisions often arise together, they punish different conduct.

| Section 66C                                                                                                                                         | Section 66D                                                                                                |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on **using another person's identity or credentials**.                                                                                      | Focuses on **using that identity to deceive or cheat someone**.                                            |
| The offence is complete once another person's electronic signature, password, or unique identification feature is dishonestly or fraudulently used. | The offence requires **personation** and **cheating** through a computer resource or communication device. |
| Protects digital identity.                                                                                                                          | Protects people from online deception and fraud.                                                           |

A simple way to remember the distinction is:

### Section 66C is about stealing an identity. Section 66D is about using that identity to cheat someone.

The same incident may involve both offences.

For example, a cybercriminal who steals another person's banking credentials (**Section 66C**) and then uses those credentials to fraudulently transfer money (**Section 66D**) may be prosecuted under both provisions.

## Section 66E – Violation of Privacy

The increasing use of smartphones, hidden cameras, and social media has made privacy violations easier than ever before.

Section 66E was introduced by the **Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008** to address this growing problem.

The provision makes it a criminal offence to intentionally capture, publish, or transmit images of another person's **private area** without their consent, where the person has a **reasonable expectation of privacy**.

The punishment is:

- **imprisonment of up to three years**;
- **a fine of up to ₹2 lakh**; or
- **both**.

### What is a "private area"?

The Act defines a private area to include:

- the genitals;
- the pubic area;
- the buttocks; and
- the female breast,

whether uncovered or covered only by undergarments.

The provision therefore protects against much more than complete nudity.

### What is a "reasonable expectation of privacy"?

Whether a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy depends on the circumstances.

For example, individuals would ordinarily expect privacy when they are:

- using a restroom;
- changing clothes in a trial room;
- inside a hotel room;
- receiving medical treatment; or
- in another private setting where they would not reasonably expect to be photographed or recorded.

Capturing or sharing images taken in such situations without consent may amount to an offence under Section 66E.

### Consent is the key

The central element of Section 66E is **consent**.

Even if a person knows they are being photographed, that does not necessarily mean they have consented to the image being shared or published.

Similarly, sending the image privately to a single individual may still amount to an offence.

The law does not require the image to be widely circulated—**even a one-to-one transmission can violate the victim's privacy.**

This reflects the broader recognition of privacy as a fundamental right under **_K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India_**, where the Supreme Court affirmed that informational and bodily privacy form part of the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 of the Constitution.

### Why these provisions matter

Technology has changed the nature of crime.

Many offences today no longer involve breaking into homes or stealing physical documents. Instead, they involve stolen passwords, hacked accounts, fake identities, online impersonation, and unauthorised sharing of intimate images.

Sections 66B to 66E recognise this shift. Rather than protecting computers alone, they protect the people who use them by criminalising some of the most common forms of digital exploitation and abuse.

## The 2026 Amendment: Regulating Synthetic Media and Deepfakes

On 10 February 2026, MeitY notified the **Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026**, which took effect on **20 February 2026**. These amendments update the 2021 Intermediary Rules discussed above and represent **India's first dedicated legal framework for AI-generated content and deepfakes**. Rather than regulating AI technology itself, the amendment regulates how AI-generated content is created, identified and distributed online — directly affecting the safe harbour analysis under Section 79.

### What counts as "synthetically generated information"?

The amendment introduces a new legal term: **synthetically generated information**, or synthetic media. In simple terms, this refers to AI-generated or AI-manipulated audio, images or videos that are made to look or sound real — for instance, a convincing deepfake video of a politician saying something they never said, or an AI-generated voice recording impersonating someone.

Earlier draft rules would have covered almost any content modified using AI, which many experts warned was far too broad. The final version narrows this considerably, focusing only on AI-generated content that **appears authentic and could mislead** people into believing it is real.

<Example title='Excluded from "Synthetic Media"'>

The rules specifically exclude everyday, non-deceptive uses of AI, so that routine editing tools are not swept into the definition:

- Improving image or audio quality

- Translating content

- Editing videos without changing their meaning

- Formatting documents or presentations

- Accessibility tools that make content easier to read or understand

</Example>

### Who has to follow these rules?

The rules apply to **intermediaries** — the same broad category discussed above — including social media platforms, search engines, online marketplaces, cloud and web-hosting providers, and telecom companies. **Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs)** — platforms with more than five million registered users in India — face additional, stricter obligations because of the scale at which they distribute information.

### What do platforms now have to do?

Platforms offering AI tools for creating or modifying content can no longer simply provide the technology and step away from responsibility. They must take **reasonable technical measures** to stop users from creating or sharing unlawful AI-generated content, and if AI-generated content is permitted on the platform, it must generally be clearly identified.

This can be done through visible labels, embedded metadata, digital watermarks, unique identifiers, or other technical methods. For videos and images, the label must be obvious to viewers; for audio, there should be a spoken disclosure at the start so listeners know immediately that the recording was AI-generated. An earlier draft required labels to occupy 10% of the content, but after industry feedback the government removed this rigid requirement — platforms now have flexibility in how they display labels, provided they remain prominent and cannot be easily removed or tampered with.

### Extra responsibilities for large social media platforms

SSMIs must require users to declare, at the point of upload, whether their content is AI-generated. Critically, the platform cannot simply rely on that declaration — it must also use automated tools or other technical systems to verify whether the declaration is accurate. If either the user or the platform's own verification confirms the content is AI-generated, the platform must clearly label it before allowing it to remain online. Responsibility is therefore shared between users and platforms, rather than resting entirely on either one.

### Faster takedown timelines

The amendment significantly shortens the time platforms have to remove illegal content. For particularly sensitive material — such as non-consensual intimate images or deepfakes — the compressed timelines are especially aggressive.

| Requirement                                                   | Earlier → New Rule               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Government or court-ordered takedown                          | **36 hours → 3 hours**           |
| Removal of non-consensual intimate deepfakes (on complaint)   | **24 hours → 2 hours**           |
| General grievance resolution                                  | **15 days → 7 days**             |
| Certain urgent grievances (e.g. CSAM, non-consensual imagery) | **No fixed timeline → 36 hours** |
| Reminder to users about platform rules                        | **Once a year → Every 3 months** |

### Why does safe harbour matter here?

As explained above, **safe harbour** under Section 79 shields intermediaries from liability for user-generated content, provided they meet due diligence requirements. The 2026 amendment makes clear that platforms risk **losing this protection** if they:

- Knowingly allow unlawful AI-generated content to remain online
- Fail to comply with the new labelling requirements, or
- Ignore their obligations under the amended Rules

<Note label="Note">

Losing safe harbour exposes a platform to **direct legal liability** for user-generated content — a materially more serious consequence than simply receiving a regulatory direction to take content down.

</Note>

### Why these rules matter

The 2026 amendment shows MeitY responding to public feedback: compared to the earlier draft, the government narrowed the definition of synthetic media and abandoned the rigid watermarking requirement, reducing the risk of over-regulating harmless everyday AI use. At the same time, it places considerably greater responsibility on online platforms, which must now identify AI-generated content, label it clearly, detect unlawful deepfakes, respond to complaints within hours, and maintain moderation systems capable of meeting these demanding timelines. Whether the shortened takedown deadlines prove practical in operation remains to be seen — many platforms may need significant upgrades to their moderation and compliance infrastructure to keep pace.
